Rock physics, fluids, and AVO — from the ground up

A seismic response is evidence, not an answer

"Bright spot means gas" is a useful starting point and a dangerous rule. This site lets you build the rock yourself — its lithology, its porosity, what's filling its pores — and watch what that rock actually does to a seismic trace.

You'll find, quickly, that more than one rock can make the same trace. That's not a bug in the physics. It's the reason seismic interpretation is a process of building plausible models and ruling things out — not reading an answer off a screen.

The core loop

Rock model  →  rock properties  →  impedance
 →  reflectivity  →  synthetic trace  →  noise  →  interpretation

Change one thing. Watch how far it travels down the chain.

What do I know?

observation

Start with what the seismic data actually shows — amplitude, polarity, shape — and nothing you haven't verified.

What could explain it?

candidate models

Porosity, fluid, and lithology can each produce a similar-looking response. List the plausible rocks before you pick one.

What can I conclude?

uncertainty, stated

Some models are ruled out. Some aren't. Saying so honestly is the actual skill this site is teaching.

What this site is, and isn't

These modules are teaching models, built to make ideas visible. The rock physics is a real, simplified Gassmann fluid-substitution model — not a shortcut past the physics — but every module leaves things out on purpose (angle-independent reflectivity in Module 01, for instance) so the one idea in focus stays visible. Use them to build intuition, not to evaluate a real prospect.

An interactive companion to AASPI's seismic-fundamentals teaching site, School of Geosciences, University of Oklahoma. Free to use or adapt in your own courses with citation.
