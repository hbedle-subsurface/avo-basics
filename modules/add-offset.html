Module 03  ·  Amplitude versus offset

Add offset

Everything so far used one number: the amplitude of a reflection recorded straight down. Modules 01 and 02 showed how little that one number pins down. A seismic survey does not record one trace per point, though — it records dozens, at increasing distance from the source, and the reflection is not the same on all of them. That variation is a second measurement, and it is sensitive to something the first one was completely blind to.

1Offset is not angle

The conversion everyone does in their head, and gets wrong

AVO is a statement about angle: how steeply the wave strikes the interface. A recording crew measures offset: how far the receiver is from the source. Those are different quantities and the step between them is where a lot of quiet error gets in.

The obvious conversion draws a straight line from source down to the reflector and back up, which gives tan θ = (x/2)/z. That would be right if the earth had one velocity. It does not. Velocity rises with depth, so by Snell's law the ray is bent continuously, and the angle it finally makes at the reservoir is larger than the straight line suggests. The left panel draws both rays so you can see the gap.

The right panel is the consequence: angle against offset, for three depths. Two things to take from it. The curve flattens, so doubling the offset does not double the angle. And the same offset is a completely different angle at a different depth — drag the depth slider and watch your widest angle collapse. A 3000 m offset reaches 64° on a 1200 m target and 35° on a 3200 m one, which is why deep AVO is hard for reasons that have nothing to do with the rocks.

The straight-ray error always runs the same way — it under-reads the angle — so a gradient estimated against straight-ray angles is systematically too large. That is a bias rather than a random error, and it does not average out over a survey.

2What offset lets you see

One property, invisible at zero offset, dominant at thirty degrees

A wave arriving straight down is pure compression. It squeezes the rock and the rock pushes back, and how hard it pushes back is the impedance. Nothing about the rock's resistance to shearing comes into it, because nothing is being sheared.

Tilt the ray and that stops being true. An obliquely incident P wave sets the interface moving partly sideways, which shears it, and part of the energy converts into a shear wave. From that moment the shear velocities of both rocks appear in the answer.

How to read the curve. Every one of these plots is a gather, redrawn. A gather is a set of traces recorded at increasing distance from the source, and each trace has its own reflection strength. Line those strengths up left to right and you get the curve. So the left edge of any curve on this page is the trace recorded almost straight above the reservoir, the right edge is the one recorded from furthest away, and the height of the line is simply how strong the reflection is on that trace. The header at the top of the page shows both pictures side by side, and so does every step from here on.

The panels below are the cleanest demonstration of what offset adds in this whole site. They take one rock and change only its shear velocity, by fifteen percent either way, leaving Vp and density exactly alone. Look at the left edge of the curves: all three start from the identical point, because at zero offset Vs has no effect whatsoever. Then follow them right. By thirty degrees they are far apart.

The gathers beside them say the same thing in the form you would meet it on a workstation. The near traces of all three rocks are indistinguishable. The far traces are obviously different. That difference is the only place the shear information lives.

That is the whole reason AVO exists. Vs is the property that responds differently to a gas fill than Vp does, and normal incidence cannot see it at all.

Density deserves a word too. It appears at every angle, including zero, so it stays tangled up with Vp in the near-offset amplitude and cannot be separated from it there either. What offset adds is specifically the shear information, and only that.

3The curve your rock actually makes

Exact Zoeppritz, and the gather it produces

Now the full calculation for the rock you have built. The curve on the left is the exact solution — Zoeppritz's equations, solved rather than approximated — and the panel beside it is the gather that curve produces once each trace is convolved with a wavelet.

Take a brine sand at 20% porosity first. It starts positive and falls, crossing zero somewhere out on the far offsets. Now switch to gas. It starts negative and gets more negative with angle, which is the response people picture when they say "a bright spot". Then set the porosity to 10% and keep the gas: it starts positive and falls steeply. Same fluid, opposite-looking gather.

Two habits are worth forming here. Read the starting value and the slope as separate facts, because they carry different information and module 04 is entirely about that split. And notice that a curve which changes sign partway out looks like a reflection that fades and reappears on a gather — an event vanishing at mid offset is a signal, not a processing problem.

The base reflection is computed separately rather than assumed to be the mirror of the top. At normal incidence an interface and its reverse do give exactly opposite coefficients, but that symmetry breaks as soon as the ray tilts, so the base of the reservoir has an AVO response of its own. On a real gather the two interfere.

4The shortcuts, and where they fail

The more complete approximation is not the more accurate one

Nobody inverts Zoeppritz's equations in practice. They are exact but opaque — four coupled equations with no useful intuition in them — so the working forms are linearizations. The two-term Shuey approximation, R(θ) ≈ R₀ + G sin²θ, is the one almost all AVO analysis is built on, and the three-term version adds a curvature term that matters only at wide angles.

Both are drawn below against the exact answer, with the error underneath. Out to about twenty degrees they are all fine. Past thirty they separate, and here is the part that catches people: the three-term approximation is often further from the truth than the two-term one. Its extra term carries tan²θ, which grows without limit, so at wide angles it does not gently lose accuracy — it diverges.

The practical rule that follows is not "use more terms". It is that the two-term form is trustworthy over the angle range where most surveys have usable data anyway, and that fitting a third term to a noisy gather with a limited angle range usually buys less than it costs. Module 05 shows how much less.

The three-term form is not wrong. It is the correct linearization to third order, and over a wide, clean, well-conditioned angle range it does better. The failure is one of conditioning rather than of algebra: the term it adds is small where the data is good and enormous where the data is poor, so it draws its estimate from exactly the samples you trust least.

5What the second measurement buys

Two ties from the earlier modules, one broken and one not

Modules 01 and 02 each ended with two rocks that produced identical zero-offset amplitudes. Now they can be tested properly, because there is a second measurement to test them with.

The first panel asks the only question that matters: how far apart do the two rocks get, and is that further apart than the noise? It plots the gap between each pair against angle. Above the shaded band the difference is measurable; inside it, it is not. One line climbs out. The other does not. The two panels beside it are the four rocks themselves, drawn as gathers, so you can look at what the two answers mean.

The middle panel is module 01's pair: an oil sand at 30% porosity against a plain brine sand at 35%. Their zero-offset amplitudes differ by 0.0001. Their Vp/Vs values differ by 0.21, which is a lot, so the curves pull apart steadily and by thirty degrees the gap is 0.0256 — comfortably outside a clean or moderate noise level. Offset breaks this tie.

The right panel is module 02's pair: a 30% porosity sand full of gas against a 34% porosity sand with only a tenth as much gas in it. Here Vp/Vs differs by 0.07 rather than 0.21, and the curves stay close. At thirty degrees the gap is 0.0123, which is smaller than the moderate noise level. Offset does not break this tie, and no amount of processing will, because the information is not in the data.

This is the honest summary of what adding offset achieves. It converts one measurement into two, and the second one sees shear. Ambiguities that involve a change in Vp/Vs get resolved. Ambiguities that do not, are not touched. Knowing which kind you are looking at, before you commit to an AVO study, is worth more than any amount of care in the processing.

WWhy it matters

Where the angle question decides the outcome

Almost every practical AVO decision reduces to a question about angle range, and most of them get answered by habit instead.

  • Survey design. Whether a proposed cable length gives useful AVO on the target is a step-1 calculation, and it has to be done at the target's depth rather than in general. The same acquisition that was excellent on a shallow prospect can be useless two seconds deeper.
  • Deciding whether AVO is worth doing. Step 5 is the test. If the ambiguity you are trying to resolve involves a change in Vp/Vs, offset will help. If it does not — and fizz gas does not — then an AVO study will produce a number, it will look like an answer, and it will not be one.
  • Reading angle stacks. A near stack and a far stack are two samples of the curve in step 3. Knowing what angles actually went into each one, after ray bending and after whatever mute was applied, is the difference between comparing two measurements and comparing two arbitrary averages.
  • Choosing a formulation. The choice between two and three terms is not about rigour, as step 4 shows. It is about whether the angle range and the noise can support a third parameter.

The failure this module is built against is treating angle as a detail of the processing rather than as the physical variable the whole method depends on. Amplitudes get extracted against offset, plotted against sin²θ using a straight-ray conversion, fitted, and interpreted — and the systematic angle error from step 1 propagates straight into the gradient, in a known direction, without ever announcing itself.

There is a second habit worth breaking, which is treating the exact solution as the rigorous one and the approximation as the compromise. Step 4 shows a case where the more complete approximation is further from the truth over the range that matters. Exactness in one part of a calculation earns nothing if the inputs to it are uncertain, and on a real gather the angles, the wavelet and the amplitudes all are.

The useful summary of this module is a question to ask before starting: what is the Vp/Vs difference between the two explanations I am trying to separate, and does my angle range turn that difference into a measurable amplitude difference? Both halves are computable in advance, from a well or from a rock physics model, and neither needs the seismic.

Exercises

  1. Lose your far angles by burying the target. Set the longest offset to 3000 m and the depth to 1200 m, and read the angle at your longest offset. Then walk the depth down to 3200 m, reading it again at each stop.
    HintAt 1200 m the 3000 m offset reaches 64.3°; at 2000 m it reaches 48.4°; at 3200 m it reaches 35.1°. The survey did not change. Nothing about the acquisition changed. The angle range available for AVO fell by nearly thirty degrees purely because the target got deeper, and step 5 shows that separating two rocks depends almost entirely on how wide an angle you can reach. This is why the same processing sequence produces convincing AVO on one horizon and noise on another.
  2. Measure the cost of the straight-ray shortcut. At 2000 m depth, compare the true angle at the longest offset with the straight-ray estimate, at 1500 m, 3000 m and 4500 m offset.
    HintAt 1500 m the true angle is 26.4° against a straight-ray estimate of 20.6°. At 3000 m it is 48.4° against 36.9°. At 4500 m it is 65.4° against 48.4°. The error grows with offset and always in the same direction — the shortcut under-reads. Because the gradient is a slope against sin²θ, using angles that are systematically too small makes the fitted gradient systematically too steep. It is a bias, not scatter, so acquiring more data does not reduce it.
  3. Confirm that Vs is invisible at zero offset. With the page at its defaults — a gas sand at 30% porosity — read the spread across the three curves in step 2 at 0° and then at 30°.
    HintAt 0° the spread is exactly zero — not small, zero — because the three rocks share a Vp and a density, and normal-incidence reflectivity depends on nothing else. At 30° the same three rocks span 0.0671, and a brine sand at the same porosity spans 0.0710. Changing one property by fifteen percent moved the near-offset amplitude by nothing at all, and moved the far-offset amplitude by about a third of the entire gas anomaly of module 01. That is the second measurement, and it is measuring something genuinely new rather than a better version of the first.
  4. Find where the shortcut with more terms does worse. With a gas sand at 30% porosity, read the two-term and three-term errors at 20°, 30° and 40°.
    HintAt 20° the two-term error is −0.0098 and the three-term is −0.0111, so they are comparable and both small. At 30° they are −0.0167 and −0.0237. At 40° they are −0.0189 and −0.0434 — the three-term error is more than twice the two-term one. The extra term carries tan²θ, which runs away, so the more complete approximation diverges faster than the simpler one it was supposed to improve. Adding parameters to a fit is not the same as adding accuracy.
  5. Decide which tie offset can break. In step 5, read both separations at 30° and compare each against the moderate noise level.
    HintThe oil-against-brine pair separates by 0.0256 at 30°, against a moderate noise level of 0.015: measurable. The gas-against-fizz pair separates by 0.0123, which is below that noise level: not measurable. The reason is visible in the rocks rather than in the seismic. Oil and brine differ in Vp/Vs by 0.21; full gas and fizz gas differ by 0.07. Offset measures shear, so it resolves ambiguities in proportion to how much shear behaviour differs between them, and the fizz problem barely involves any. You could have predicted both outcomes from the rock physics before recording a single trace.

Key points

  • Offset is not angle, and the usual conversion under-reads. The straight-ray shortcut gives 36.9° where the true angle at a 2000 m target with 3000 m offset is 48.4°. The error runs one way, so it biases every gradient computed from it in the same direction.
  • The same offset is a different angle at a different depth. A 3000 m offset reaches 64.3° on a 1200 m target and 35.1° on a 3200 m one. Angle range is a property of the target as much as of the survey.
  • What offset adds is shear. Change a rock's Vs by fifteen percent either way and the zero-offset amplitude does not move at all, while the amplitude at 30° spans 0.0671. Normal incidence is blind to Vs; oblique incidence is not.
  • The three-term approximation is not the safer one. At 40° its error is more than twice the two-term error, because the term it adds carries tan²θ and diverges. More parameters is not more accuracy.
  • Offset resolves ambiguities that involve Vp/Vs, and only those. Oil against brine, which differ by 0.21 in Vp/Vs, separate by 0.0256 at 30° and can be told apart. Gas against fizz gas, differing by 0.07, separate by 0.0123 and cannot.
  • You can work out in advance whether AVO will help. Both halves of the question — the Vp/Vs contrast between your competing explanations, and the angle range your survey reaches on this target — are computable before any seismic is examined.

MMethod, simplifications and references

What is computed, and where it departs from production software

The rock physics is module 01's, unchanged. Voigt-Reuss-Hill mineral mixing, a Dvorkin-Nur soft-sand dry frame at 40% critical porosity and 23 MPa, Batzle-Wang fluids mixed by the Reuss average, Gassmann for the saturated modulus, and Castagna-Gardner for the bounding shale. Module 01's Method tab lists the assumptions and they all apply here.

Reflection coefficients are exact. The curves are the Zoeppritz solution for the plane-wave PP reflection, obtained by solving the four-by-four system rather than by any approximation. The Shuey curves in step 4 are drawn only to be compared against it. The base of the reservoir is computed as its own interface rather than assumed to be the negative of the top, because that symmetry holds only at normal incidence.

Ray geometry is exact too, for the velocity model assumed. The overburden is taken as V(z) = 1600 + 0.6z m/s, a compacting clastic section. For a linear gradient the rays are circular arcs with a closed-form offset and traveltime, so the incidence angle at the target is found by solving for the ray parameter that arrives at the requested offset, not by an approximation. The straight-ray comparison curve is tan θ = (x/2)/z.

Depth changes only the geometry here. Moving the depth slider changes the traveltime, the RMS velocity and therefore the angle at a given offset. It deliberately does not change the effective pressure acting on the rock, which stays at 23 MPa. In reality a deeper reservoir is also a stiffer one, and its anomaly would weaken for that reason as well. Both effects are real; separating them is what makes the angle lesson legible.

Where this departs from production practice.

  • The gathers are already flattened. Normal moveout has been removed and no stretch has been applied. Real NMO stretches the far-offset wavelet to lower frequency, which changes the amplitude of a tuned event with offset for reasons that have nothing to do with the rocks. That is a module 05 subject and it is one of the largest sources of false AVO.
  • Amplitudes are plane-wave coefficients only. No geometric spreading, no transmission loss through the overburden, no attenuation, and no source or receiver directivity — every one of which varies with offset and has to be corrected before the remaining variation means anything.
  • The overburden is isotropic. Real shales are not. Anisotropy changes the moveout and the angle-dependent reflectivity, and ignoring it produces a gradient error that can be as large as the fluid effect being looked for.
  • One interface pair, one wavelet, no interference from above or below, and the reservoir thickness is fixed at 45 m so that tuning does not vary across the exercises. Module 02 covers what tuning does to an amplitude.
  • Converted waves are computed but not shown. The Zoeppritz solve returns the PS coefficients as well; only PP is used here.
  • Angles beyond critical are left blank rather than guessed. Where the transmitted wave turns, the reflection coefficient becomes complex and the real-arithmetic solution stops being meaningful. The curve simply ends.
