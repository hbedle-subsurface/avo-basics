Module 01  ·  The foundation

Build a rock, make a seismic trace

One reservoir, sandwiched in shale. Choose what it's made of, what's filling its pores, and how noisy your data is — then follow the chain from rock to reflection to trace. Nothing here is a trick yet. The trick comes once you notice how many different rocks can make the same trace.

A seismic response is evidence, not an answer

It's tempting to learn a shortcut like "bright spot = gas" and treat it as a rule. It's not a rule — it's a pattern that sometimes holds. A bright reflection can come from gas, yes. It can also come from a rock that's simply more porous, or from a change in lithology, or from some combination of all three.

This module lets you build the rock yourself, so you can watch several different causes produce a similar-looking seismic response. Keep asking three questions as you go: What do I know? What could explain it? What can I actually conclude?

The chain this module builds

1 · Rock model. A reservoir bed between two shales. You set its lithology, porosity, and what fills the pore space.

2 · Rock properties. Porosity and fluid change the rock's stiffness and density — which set its velocity.

3 · Impedance → reflectivity → trace. Velocity and density combine into impedance; the impedance contrast at each boundary sets the reflection; the reflections, convolved with a wavelet, are what you'd actually record — noise and all.

Change one thing at a time and watch how far the change travels down that chain — and, just as tellingly, how much of the final trace it doesn't explain.

Try this — change one thing at a time

  1. Raise the porosity. Now drag the porosity slider up toward 30%. Watch panel 2: Vp, Vs and density all drop, because a more porous rock has less solid mineral holding it together. Impedance drops with them, the reflection coefficients grow, and the trace gets brighter — without a single drop of fluid changing.
  2. Now keep the porosity fixed and change the fluid instead. Switch the fluid from brine to gas. A different physical change — fluid, not porosity — and yet you can get a similarly brighter reflection. Two different stories, a similar seismic outcome. That's the beginning of the site's central lesson: a seismic response is evidence, not an answer.
  3. Add noise without changing the rock at all. Leave the rock alone and step the noise control from Clean to Noisy. Panels 1–3 don't move — the earth hasn't changed. Only panel 4 changes, because noise is something that happens to the data, not to the rock. Even a perfectly known geological model produces an imperfect trace.
  4. The trick, live: two different rocks, one amplitude. Note panel 3's reflection coefficient, then and compare. Reservoir A is a brine-filled sand with somewhat higher porosity. Reservoir B is a lower-porosity sand carrying some gas. Their porosities aren't even wildly different — and their reflection coefficients come out nearly identical anyway. Looking at panel 3 or panel 4 alone, you cannot tell which reservoir has the gas. That's not a flaw in this tool; it's the actual physics, and it's why interpreters lean on more than amplitude alone.

Why this happens: fluid substitution

When you change the fluid, you're not changing the rock's mineral frame at all — the grains and how they're packed stay exactly the same. What changes is how the pore fluid resists being squeezed. Gas squeezes easily and barely adds any weight; brine resists being squeezed more and is heavier. That's essentially the whole story of fluid substitution: same rock, different pore fluid, different stiffness and density, different velocity.

The equation behind it is called Gassmann's equation, and you don't need it to use this module — the panels already show you the effect. If you want the mechanics, they're in the notes at the bottom of the page. Notice, too, that the fluid never affects the rock's shear stiffness (Vs) at all — only Vp moves much on a fluid swap. That's one reason Vp/Vs is such a useful clue when it's available.

Method, assumptions, and where to read more

The reservoir sits between two fixed shale layers (Vp 2450 m/s, density 2.38 g/cc — the same shale used in the "Where a seismic trace comes from" module on the seismic-fundamentals site) and is 50 m thick, far enough above tuning at these frequencies that its top and base stay separate reflections; thin-bed interference is the subject of its own module elsewhere on this site's sibling.

The reservoir's dry-rock frame is modeled with a simplified critical-porosity relationship: stiffness falls off roughly linearly from the mineral's own modulus at zero porosity to zero at a "critical" porosity where the grain framework can no longer support itself (0.40 for sandstone, 0.55 for shale here) — a teaching simplification of the idea behind Nur et al.'s critical-porosity model. Gassmann's equation then adds the pore fluid back in:

Ksat = Kdry + (1 − Kdry/Kmin)² / [ φ/Kfl + (1−φ)/Kmin − Kdry/Kmin² ]

with the shear modulus Gsat = Gdry — fluid substitution leaves shear stiffness untouched, which is why Vs barely reacts to the fluid controls. Brine and gas are held at fixed representative reservoir-condition values; oil's density and modulus vary with the API gravity slider using simplified trends rather than the full pressure/temperature-dependent Batzle & Wang correlations. Mixed brine/hydrocarbon saturations use a Reuss (uniform-mixing) average of the fluid modulus. As in the sibling site, the trace is built as a sum of shifted, scaled wavelets rather than a sample-grid convolution, so reflector timing is exact.

Left out deliberately, for this first module: offset/angle-dependent reflectivity (that's AVO itself, later on this site), transmission loss, internal multiples, and attenuation. All of them would change amplitudes further without changing the lesson.
