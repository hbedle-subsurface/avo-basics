Module 04  ·  Amplitude versus offset

Intercept, gradient and the classes

Module 03 turned one amplitude into a curve. Nobody carries a curve around for every sample in a survey, so the curve gets summarized by two numbers: where it starts, and how steeply it falls. Those two numbers are what AVO analysis actually works with, and the crossplot they live in is where the well-known Classes I to IV come from. This module builds that crossplot, and then shows that the classes are lines somebody drew on it.

1A curve becomes two numbers

And the second one depends on how you measured it

Module 03's curve bends. That is inconvenient, because storing a bent curve for every sample in a survey is not practical, and because a bend is hard to interpret. So AVO analysis does something simple: it plots the amplitude not against angle but against sin²θ, on which the curve straightens out almost completely, and then fits a straight line to it.

A straight line has two numbers in it. Where it crosses the left-hand edge is the intercept, written R₀ — the amplitude the reflection would have at zero offset, which is exactly the number modules 01 and 02 were about. How steeply it tilts is the gradient, G. Together they are the whole of what most AVO work uses.

Now for the part that is usually left out. The gradient is not a property of the rock. It is a property of the rock and of the angle range you fitted over, because the real curve is not quite straight. Drag the angles used for the fit slider and watch G move while the rock does not. Two people can analyse the same gather, do nothing wrong, and report different gradients because one of them muted at 30° and the other at 40°.

The intercept barely moves when you change the fit range, because every fit is anchored near zero offset where the curve is genuinely straight. The gradient moves a great deal. That asymmetry is worth remembering whenever two gradient volumes disagree: the first thing to check is not the rocks, it is what angles went into each one.

2Every rock becomes a dot

The crossplot, and how to read one

Once a rock is two numbers, you can put it on a graph. How to read the picture on the left: it is not a map and it is not a cross-section. Each dot is one rock. Its position left to right is that rock's intercept — what its near trace looks like. Its position up and down is the gradient — how much the reflection changes as you move out to the far traces. The circle is the rock currently on your sliders.

The grey dots are brine-filled rocks, at every porosity, clay content and shale velocity this page can build. The coloured ones hold oil or gas. The value of the picture is that it shows a whole population at once, which is what you have when you crossplot a real survey: one dot per sample, hundreds of thousands of them.

The three short arrows on your rock are there to give the picture a sense of direction. Each one shows where the dot would move if you changed a single thing: raised the porosity, swapped the fluid for gas, or put a faster shale on top. They are computed by actually making that change and seeing where the point goes, so they point wherever the rock physics says they should rather than wherever a textbook figure says.

Two rocks can share an intercept and differ in gradient. The panel on the right shows exactly that pair — your rock, and the wet rock whose near trace matches it most closely. Look at the near traces: the same. Look at the far traces: not the same. Everything the gradient contributes is in that difference.

3The background trend

An anomaly is a departure from something, and this is the something

The grey dots are not scattered at random. Wet rocks fall along a line, because their Vp, Vs and density are related to each other in a fairly consistent way, whatever their porosity or clay content. That line is the background trend, and it is drawn on the crossplot below.

Once the trend is there, the useful question stops being "where is this dot" and becomes "how far off the trend is it". A rock sitting on the trend is behaving like the wet rocks around it, whatever its intercept happens to be. A rock a long way off the trend is doing something the wet rocks do not do — and a pore fluid is the usual reason.

This is why an absolute gradient threshold is a poor detector and distance from the trend is a good one. It also explains a common frustration: a trend fitted in one interval does not transfer to another, because the rocks are different, so an anomaly cut-off carried across a survey quietly stops meaning the same thing.

4The classes are regions on this plot

Drawn by people, not by the rocks

Class I, Class II, Class III and Class IV are the most quoted vocabulary in AVO, and the crossplot is where they come from. Rutherford and Williams sorted gas sands by where their intercept sat: strongly positive, near zero, or strongly negative. Castagna later added a fourth for soft sands whose reflection gets weaker with offset rather than stronger.

The regions are shaded on the plot below. Notice what they are: rectangles on a graph. Nothing physical happens as a dot crosses from one into the next. The boundary near zero intercept is drawn at ±0.03 here and different books put it in different places, because there is no natural place to put it.

The middle panel is a summary you can come back to. Each row gives the signs of the intercept and gradient, what the gather does as you move out to the far offsets, and a small sketch of the curve. Every row is generated by going and finding a real rock in this page's own parameter space that lands in that class, so the sketches cannot drift away from the physics.

The panel on the right makes the point another way. Both cards are gas sands at full saturation under the same shale. One is Class I and one is Class III. If the classes described kinds of rock, that could not happen.

The classes remain useful as shorthand. "A Class II response" tells a colleague quickly that the near traces are weak and the far traces are not, which is genuinely worth saying, and Class II in particular carries a real warning: a reflection that is nearly invisible on the near offsets is easy to miss altogether on a full stack. The trouble starts when the shorthand is treated as a measurement.

5One rock, three classes

Change the shale and nothing else

Set a gas sand at 20% porosity, then move the shale velocity slider from one end to the other. The reservoir does not change. Its porosity, its clay content, its fluid and its saturation all stay exactly where they are. Watch the dot travel across the crossplot, and watch the class label under it change.

Under a slow shale the sand is harder than its surroundings, so the near trace is a peak and the response is Class I. Under the default shale the two impedances nearly match, the near trace almost vanishes and it is Class II. Under a fast shale the sand is the softer rock, the near trace is a clear trough, and it is Class III — the textbook bright spot.

The three gathers on the right are the same gas sand every time. So a class is not a description of a reservoir. It is a description of a contrast, and half of that contrast belongs to a rock nobody is trying to characterise — which is exactly what module 02 found from the other direction, when the shale you had not measured widened the admissible porosity by nine units.

The practical consequence is that "we are looking for a Class III response" is not a prospecting criterion on its own. What class your target produces depends on the seal above it, and that can change across a field faster than the reservoir does.

That is the argument for working in distance-from-trend rather than in classes, in one line.

WWhy it matters

Where intercept and gradient earn their keep, and where they mislead

Reducing a curve to two numbers is what makes AVO practical at survey scale. Two volumes instead of a hundred angle stacks, one dot per sample on a crossplot, and anomalies that can be found by a computer rather than by eye. All of that is real value.

  • Crossplot the interval, not the sample. A dot means something only against the population it came from. Extract a window that contains enough wet rock to define a trend, or there is nothing to be anomalous with respect to.
  • Record the angle range with the gradient. Step 1 shows the gradient moving by a fifth when the fit range changes and the rock does not. A gradient volume without its angle range attached cannot be compared with anything.
  • Prefer distance from the trend to absolute thresholds. A cut-off on gradient alone will follow lithology and depth around a survey. Distance from a locally fitted trend will not, or at least not as much.
  • Treat the class as vocabulary. It is a useful way to tell a colleague what the gather looks like. It is not a measurement, and it is not a property of the reservoir.

The failure this module is built against is the one in step 5. A prospect gets described as "a Class III opportunity", the phrase gets repeated, and it hardens into a property of the reservoir. Then the seal changes across the field, the response moves to Class II, and the same sand stops looking like the thing everyone agreed to look for. Nothing about the reservoir changed. The class was never about the reservoir.

There is a quieter failure in step 1. The intercept and the gradient are usually treated as two independent measurements, and they are not — they come from one fit to one set of points, so an error in one is compensated by an error in the other. That correlation is why gradients are so much less stable than intercepts, and it is the subject of the next module.

Two questions worth asking of any crossplot before believing it: what angle range produced these gradients, and where is the wet trend? If nobody can answer the first, the gradients are not comparable. If nobody has drawn the second, the anomalies have not been defined.

Exercises

  1. Change the gradient without touching the rock. With the default gas sand at 20% porosity, read the gradient. Then move the fit range from 15° to 45°, reading it at each end, and compare with how much the intercept moved.
    HintFitted over 0–20° the gradient is −0.4107; over 0–45° it is −0.4310. That is a shift of 0.0203 — about five percent of the gradient — with the rock untouched. The intercept moves by only 0.0014 over the same change, so the fit range moved the gradient fifteen times as much as it moved the intercept. Every fit is anchored near zero offset, which is what protects the intercept; nothing plays that role for the gradient.
  2. Find the wet rock hiding behind your anomaly. Set a gas sand at 30% porosity and look at the two cards in step 2 — your rock and the wet rock with the closest intercept. Compare their near traces, then their far traces.
    HintThe closest wet rock is a brine sand at 33% porosity under a fast 3100 m/s shale. Its intercept matches the gas sand's to 0.0002, so the near traces are the same picture — they differ by 0.0001. The gradients differ by 0.3745, and by the far traces the amplitudes have pulled apart by 0.0935, which is around 780 times the near-trace difference. If you only ever looked at a near stack, or at a full stack dominated by the near offsets, you could not separate these two rocks at all. All of the discriminating power is in how the traces differ across the gather, and that is what the gradient measures.
  3. Fill the same rock three ways and watch it leave the trend. In step 3, set 30% porosity and read the distance off the wet trend with brine, then oil, then gas.
    HintBrine sits −0.0461 from the trend, oil −0.1544, and gas −0.3173. The rock frame is identical in all three; only the pore fluid changed. Note that brine is not exactly on the trend either — the wet rocks scatter about their own line by about 0.039, so a departure that size means nothing at all. Oil is about four times the wet scatter and gas about eight times, which is the honest way to say how detectable each one is: gas comfortably, oil probably, and anything smaller not at all.
  4. Put two gas sands in two different classes. In step 4, compare a gas sand at 10% porosity with one at 30%, both fully saturated, both under a 2700 m/s shale.
    HintThe 10% sand has an intercept of +0.1814 and lands in Class I; the 30% sand has −0.1610 and lands in Class III. Same fluid, same saturation, same shale, opposite ends of the classification. What separated them was porosity, which is a property of the reservoir but not of its fluid. The classes sort responses, and a response is made by two rocks and a fluid together.
  5. Move one sand through three classes with the shale slider. Set gas, 20% porosity, and take the shale velocity from 2300 to 3100 m/s, reading the class at 2300, 2700 and 3100.
    HintAt 2300 m/s the intercept is +0.096 and the response is Class I. At 2700 m/s it is −0.004 and it is Class II. At 3100 m/s it is −0.090 and it is Class III. The reservoir was not touched once. Meanwhile the distance off the wet trend only moves between −0.210 and −0.298 across that whole span, because the fluid never changed and the fluid is what pushes a rock off the trend. So the class travelled through three categories while the thing you actually wanted to know stayed put. That is the clearest statement in this site of why the classes are a description of a contrast rather than of a reservoir.

Key points

  • Plotting against sin²θ straightens the curve, and a straight line is two numbers. The intercept is the zero-offset amplitude that modules 01 and 02 were about; the gradient is how fast it changes with offset. Almost all AVO analysis runs on that pair.
  • The gradient depends on the angle range you fitted over. On the default rock it moves by about five percent between a 20° and a 45° fit — fifteen times as much as the intercept moves. A gradient reported without its angle range cannot be compared with another one.
  • A crossplot is a population, not a picture of the ground. Each dot is one rock: intercept across, gradient up the side. Its value is that it shows both numbers at once, and a gradient means nothing without the intercept it came with.
  • Wet rocks fall on a trend, and that trend is what makes an anomaly anomalous. The wet rocks here follow G = −0.141 − 1.171·R₀ with a scatter of about 0.039. A departure smaller than that scatter is not evidence of anything.
  • Fluid is what pushes a rock off the trend. The same 30% porosity frame sits −0.0461 off the trend with brine, −0.1544 with oil and −0.3173 with gas.
  • The classes are rectangles somebody drew. A gas sand at 20% porosity is Class I under a 2300 m/s shale, Class II at 2700 and Class III at 3100, with the reservoir untouched throughout, while its distance off the wet trend barely moves. A class describes a contrast, and half of that contrast belongs to the seal.

MMethod, simplifications and references

What is computed, and where it departs from production software

The rock physics is module 01's, unchanged, and the reflection coefficients are exact Zoeppritz as in module 03. Module 01's Method tab lists the assumptions in the rock model and they all apply here.

The intercept and gradient are fitted, not derived. They come from an ordinary least-squares straight line through the exact reflection coefficients plotted against sin²θ, sampled every degree from zero to the angle on the fit-range slider. That is what an AVO processor does with real amplitudes. It is not the same as reading Shuey's analytic R₀ and G off the rock properties: for the default rock the fitted gradient is −0.4169 against Shuey's −0.5270, a difference of more than a fifth. Shuey's expression is the tangent to the curve at zero offset, while a fit is a compromise across the whole range, and the two only agree in the limit of a very narrow range of angles. Both are called "the gradient".

The background trend is fitted live, by least squares through every brine-filled rock this page can build: porosity from 5 to 35%, clay from 0 to 30%, and shale velocity from 2300 to 3100 m/s. It comes out as G = −0.141 − 1.171·R₀ with an R² of 0.94 and a scatter of 0.039 about the line. Real background trends are fitted from well logs or from the seismic itself over an interval, and are usually less tidy than this one because real wet rocks vary in ways this model does not include.

The class boundaries are a convention and are drawn here at ±0.03 in intercept, with the III/IV split on the sign of the gradient. Rutherford and Williams defined the first three classes descriptively, by where gas sands of different consolidation happened to sit, rather than by numeric cut-offs; Castagna and Swan added Class IV later. Different texts and different companies draw the lines in different places, and no value is standard. Any dot near a boundary here should be read as "near a boundary", not as a member of one side.

Where this departs from production practice.

  • The fit is to noise-free amplitudes. The noise control shades the gathers but the intercept and gradient are fitted to the exact values, so this page shows the effect of the angle range in isolation. What noise does to the fit — and it does a great deal, mostly to the gradient — is module 05.
  • Every angle is weighted equally. Real gathers have fewer usable traces at wide angles, variable fold, and a mute that removes the widest ones, all of which weight the fit unevenly.
  • One interface, one wavelet, thickness fixed at 45 m, so tuning does not vary across the exercises and the amplitudes are the interface response rather than an event amplitude.
  • Angles are exact for the module 03 velocity model, and the fit is done against true incidence angle. Fitting against a straight-ray angle instead, which is common, biases the gradient in the direction module 03 quantified.
  • No anisotropy. In a shale-dominated overburden this is a significant omission: anisotropy contributes a term that looks exactly like a gradient and can be comparable in size to the fluid effect being sought.
  • The crossplot shows a modelled population, not a survey. A real crossplot has one dot per sample, hundreds of thousands of them, with a density that carries information this page's evenly-spaced grid of models does not reproduce.
