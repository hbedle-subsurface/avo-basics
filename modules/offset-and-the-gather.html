Module 04  ·  The measurement

Offset, angle and the gather

Module 03 ended on a rock property that exists and does not reach a normal-incidence trace. Reaching it means recording the same reflection at more than one angle, and that means understanding the recording geometry: where the angles come from, why the distance between source and receiver is not the same thing as the angle at the reflector, what the extra travel time at long offset does, and what is lost when the traces are added together.

1Sorting the shots into gathers

What a gather is, and why it is not a shot record

A survey fires a source, records at a line of receivers, moves along and fires again. One firing gives a shot record: many receivers, one source position. Each trace in it reflected at a different place in the subsurface, so a shot record mixes geometry with geology.

The traces are therefore re-sorted before anything else is done to them. The midpoint of a trace is the point on the surface halfway between its source and its receiver, and for a flat reflector it is directly above the point where that trace reflected. Collecting every trace that shares a midpoint gives a common-midpoint gather, or CMP gather: several recordings of the same piece of subsurface, at different source-receiver separations.

That separation is the offset. A CMP gather is therefore a set of traces of one reflection point, ordered by offset, and it is the object every later module works on. The number of traces in it is the fold.

2Offset is not the angle

And the shortcut is worse than it looks

Everything from module 05 onward is about how the reflection changes with the angle at which the wave meets the boundary. What a survey records is offset. The two are related, and converting between them is where a good deal of trouble starts.

Module 00 used the simplest relationship: treat the ray as a straight line, and the half-offset and the depth are two sides of a right triangle, so the angle is the arctangent of one over the other. That is exact only if the velocity is constant all the way down.

It is not. Velocity generally rises with depth as sediments compact, which is the trend module 01 built into the grain frame. A ray travelling through rising velocity bends continuously, refracting away from the vertical as it goes, so it reaches the reflector at a steeper angle than a straight line from the same source would.

The left-hand panel draws both. The right-hand panel plots the two angles against offset, and the gap between them widens the whole way. It is not a small correction: at the settings this module opens with, the shortcut is 6.6 degrees low at 2000 m offset and 10.1 degrees low at 3000 m. An analysis that uses the wrong angle is fitting the right data to the wrong axis.

3Moveout

The far traces arrive late, by a lot

A longer offset is a longer path, so the reflection arrives later. The difference between the arrival time at some offset and the arrival time at zero offset is the normal moveout, and on a gather it is the curvature of the event.

To a good approximation the arrival time follows a hyperbola: t² = t₀² + x²/V², where x is the offset, t₀ the zero-offset time, and V a velocity that describes the whole overburden. The velocity that makes this work is close to the root-mean-square velocity down to the reflector, which is not the same as the average velocity and not the same as the velocity at the reflector itself. Three different velocities are in play and confusing them is a common source of error.

The moveout is large. At the settings here the longest trace arrives several hundred milliseconds after the nearest one, which is far more than the length of the wavelet. Nothing can be compared across the gather until it is removed.

4Flattening it, and what that costs

The correction is not free

Removing the moveout means shifting every sample on every trace up by the difference between its own arrival time and the zero-offset time. Do it correctly and the event becomes flat across the gather, which is what makes the amplitudes comparable.

The shift is not the same at every time on a trace, because moveout depends on t₀ as well as on offset. Shallow samples move up further than deep ones, so the trace is not translated, it is stretched. The waveform on a far trace comes out longer and lower in frequency than the one on a near trace, and the effect grows with offset and shrinks with depth.

That matters here more than it does for most purposes. An amplitude read from a stretched far trace is not measuring the same thing as one read from an unstretched near trace, and the stretch is worst exactly where the angle range is widest and the AVO information is largest. Processing normally mutes the far traces where the stretch exceeds some threshold, which throws away the angles that carried the most.

5Stacking, and what it discards

Why AVO work is done on gathers

Once the gather is flat the usual next step is to add the traces together and divide by the fold. That is the stack, and it is the section almost every interpreter works from. It is a good thing to do: the reflection is the same on every trace and adds coherently, while random noise does not, so the signal-to-noise ratio improves roughly with the square root of the fold.

It is also the end of the road for everything this set is about. The amplitude in this gather is not constant across offset — the rocks are the shale and gas sand from modules 02 and 03, and their reflection coefficient changes with angle. Adding the traces together replaces that whole trend with its average.

The left-hand panel plots the amplitude trace by trace, which is the AVO response seen as data rather than as a curve. The right-hand panel shows the stack: one number, sitting somewhere in the middle of the trend, carrying no record that a trend existed.

Nothing is wrong with the stack. It answers a different question. A measurement that depends on how the amplitude varies with angle has to be made before the stack, on the gather, which is why AVO analysis lives where it does in a processing sequence and why a stacked volume alone cannot be used for it.

WWhy it matters

Where the geometry decides the answer

Deciding whether a target is reachable. The angle a survey achieves at a target depends on the offsets recorded and the depth of the target. A deep reservoir on a short spread may never reach a wide enough angle range for the amplitude variation to be measurable, and that is a survey design question rather than a processing one.

Converting offsets to angles correctly. Every AVO analysis needs an angle for each trace. Using the straight-ray shortcut with a real velocity gradient puts every point on the wrong part of the axis, and the error grows with offset, so it distorts the shape of the trend rather than shifting it.

Reading angle stacks. A near stack and a far stack are averages over ranges of angle, and which angles fell into which stack depends on the velocity model used to sort them. Two processing runs with different velocities produce different angle stacks from identical field data.

Knowing what the mute removed. Stretch mutes are applied routinely and they remove the far offsets first. Since those carry most of the angle information, a gather that looks clean after processing may have had the most useful part of it removed.

  1. Measure the error in the straight-ray shortcut. With the default overburden, read the straight-ray angle and the true angle at the longest offset. Then set the velocity gradient to zero and read them again.
    AnswerAt a 0.50 /s gradient the shortcut gives 36.9° where the true angle is 47.0° — it is 10.1 degrees low. With the gradient at zero the two agree exactly, because a constant velocity is the one case in which a ray really does travel in a straight line. The entire discrepancy is the gradient, and since it grows with offset it bends the shape of an AVO trend rather than simply shifting it.
  2. Find the offset you need for a given angle. Read the offset that reaches 30° at 2000 m depth, then take the target to 3500 m and read it again.
    AnswerAbout 1780 m at 2000 m depth, and about 2820 m at 3500 m. A deeper target needs a longer spread to reach the same angle, roughly in proportion to its depth. This is why deep prospects on short-offset surveys are often not candidates for amplitude work at all: the data exists, and the angle range in it does not.
  3. Compare the moveout with the wavelet. Read the moveout at the longest offset and compare it with the length of a 30 Hz wavelet, roughly 33 ms from peak to peak.
    AnswerThe moveout is 477 ms at the default settings, about fourteen times the wavelet length. The far trace and the near trace are not slightly misaligned; the event on one is nowhere near the event on the other. Nothing about amplitude can be compared across a gather until this is removed.
  4. Watch the stretch appear. Compare the waveform on the near and far traces in step 4, then read the stretch factor and the apparent frequency at the longest offset.
    AnswerThe far trace is stretched by 1.25, so a 30 Hz wavelet arrives looking like 24 Hz. The correction shifts shallow samples further than deep ones, which lengthens the waveform rather than moving it. A mute set at a stretch of 1.20 cuts in at 2679 m, where the incidence angle is 42.9°, and removes three of the twenty-four traces. Those three carry the widest angles on the gather, which are the ones with the most to say about the fluid.
  5. Stack the gather and see what is left. Read the amplitude on the near trace, on the far trace, and on the stack.
    AnswerThe amplitude runs from −0.161 on the zero-offset trace to −0.328 at 47°, so it roughly doubles in magnitude across the gather, and the stack reports −0.223. That single number is a weighted average of a trend, and nothing in it records that the trend existed or which direction it ran. A different set of offsets would average to a different number from identical rocks. Everything this set does from here on has to happen before this step.

Key points

  • A CMP gather is several recordings of one reflection point, sorted by the distance between source and receiver. That distance is the offset, and the number of traces is the fold.
  • Offset is not the incidence angle. The angle depends on the offset, the depth and the whole velocity profile above the reflector.
  • A ray in rising velocity bends. It reaches the reflector at a steeper angle than a straight line does, and the straight-ray shortcut is 10.1 degrees low at 3000 m offset here.
  • The moveout is far larger than the wavelet. Several hundred milliseconds against tens, so nothing can be compared across a gather until it is removed.
  • Removing it stretches the far traces, because the shift depends on time as well as on offset. A stretched waveform is longer and lower in frequency than the one it came from.
  • Stretch mutes remove the far offsets, which are the traces carrying the widest angles and the most information about the fluid.
  • Stacking replaces the whole trend with its average. It improves the signal-to-noise ratio and destroys the angle dependence at the same time.
  • So AVO analysis has to happen on the gather, before the stack. A stacked volume cannot be used for it, however good it looks.

MMethod, simplifications and references

What is computed, and what is idealized

The overburden is a linear velocity gradient, V(z) = V₀ + kz, which is a reasonable description of a compacting clastic section and has closed-form ray paths. The true incidence angle is obtained by finding the ray parameter whose path emerges at the requested offset, by bisection, and then reading the angle at the reflector. The straight-ray angle drawn beside it is arctan of the half-offset over the depth, which is the same shortcut module 00 used and said it would revisit here.

Moveout is the hyperbolic approximation, t² = t₀² + x²/V²rms, with the RMS velocity computed from the same gradient. This is the standard correction and it is an approximation: it is exact for a single homogeneous layer and degrades as offset approaches depth. The correction applied to the gather is done by resampling each trace at the moveout time for every output sample, which is how the stretch arises — it is a consequence of the arithmetic rather than something added afterwards.

The reflection amplitudes are exact Zoeppritz P-wave coefficients for the module 03 rock pair: a Castagna mudrock shale over a 30% porosity gas sand from the module 01 frame and the module 02 fluid, at 23 MPa and 64 °C. They are evaluated at each trace's own true incidence angle. Traces whose angle exceeds 60° are dropped, because the coefficient changes very rapidly there and the approximations used downstream have stopped holding; the readouts report the farthest trace that survived.

Idealized rather than modeled:

  • The reflector is flat and horizontal, so the midpoint really is above the reflection point. On a dipping reflector it is not, and that is what migration exists to fix.
  • The fold is uniform and the offsets are regular. Real gathers are irregular, have gaps, and vary in fold across a survey.
  • No geometrical spreading, transmission loss, absorption or multiples. Each of these changes amplitude with offset for reasons that have nothing to do with the rock, and correcting for them is a large part of what preparing gathers for AVO involves.
  • No anisotropy. In a shale-rich section the moveout is not hyperbolic even in principle, and an anisotropic correction with a third parameter is used instead.
  • No noise. What noise does to a measurement made across a gather is module 08.
