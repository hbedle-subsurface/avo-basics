Module 05  ·  Amplitude versus offset

Reading a gather you did not make

Every fit in module 04 was made to exact amplitudes over whatever angle range you asked for. Real gathers do not oblige. The amplitudes carry noise, the angle range is whatever the acquisition and the mute left you, and the two numbers you extract are estimates with error bars rather than measurements. This module puts sizes on those error bars, and then asks whether the rocks the earlier modules separated can still be told apart.

1The same rock, recorded twice

Two answers from one piece of geology

The gather in the header has noise on it. Picking the amplitude of the top reflection on each trace gives the scattered points in the panel below, and the line fitted through them gives an intercept and a gradient. That is the whole measurement, and it is what every AVO product in a survey is built from.

Press record it again. The rock has not changed by so much as a percent of porosity. The survey has not changed. Only the particular noise on this particular gather has changed, and the answer moves. The grey lines are the previous attempts, kept so you can watch them accumulate into a fan.

Notice which end of the fan is tight and which end is loose. Near zero the lines pass through nearly the same point. Out at the far offsets they spread widely. The fit is anchored at the near end and waves about at the far end, and everything else in this module follows from that one picture.

2The gradient takes almost all the damage

Same noise on every trace, very different consequences

Rather than pressing the button a hundred times, the page does it for you. The two histograms below are a thousand recordings of the same rock, showing where the recovered intercept landed and where the recovered gradient landed. Both are centred on the truth. What differs is the width.

The numbers underneath give the ratio, and it is large. At a 0–30° fit the gradient's scatter is about eight times the intercept's. Narrow the angle range and the ratio grows; widen it and the ratio shrinks. It does not depend on the noise level at all — doubling the noise doubles both scatters and leaves the ratio exactly where it was. The ratio is a property of the geometry of the fit, not of the data quality.

The reason is in step 1's picture. The intercept is read where all the lines converge; the gradient is read from how much they fan apart. A fit that is well anchored at one end is necessarily loose about its slope.

3The angle range is the lever arm

Where the mute goes decides what the gradient is worth

The gradient's uncertainty depends on how far apart your angles are spread, and on nothing else about them. That makes the mute — a processing decision, often made for reasons that have nothing to do with AVO — one of the largest controls on the quality of an AVO product.

Drag the widest angle slider and watch the curve on the left. Cutting from 40° back to 20° roughly triples the uncertainty in the gradient. Cutting to 15° multiplies it by more than six. The rock is identical throughout; so is the noise on each trace.

The panel on the right shows the other lever, which is the number of traces. It helps, but only as the square root: to halve the error you need four times as many traces. Adding fold is expensive and adding angle range is often free — it is a mute parameter — which is worth knowing before commissioning a reprocessing.

There is a limit to how far this argument goes. Module 03 showed the two-term approximation losing accuracy past about thirty degrees and the far offsets being where NMO stretch, anisotropy and residual moveout do their worst. Wider is better for the arithmetic of the fit and worse for the assumptions behind it, and the best angle range is a compromise rather than a maximum.

4The two errors are tied together

You cannot be wrong about one without being wrong about the other

Intercept and gradient are usually treated as two independent measurements. They are not. They come from one fit to one set of points, so if the noise happens to pull the fitted line up at the near end, the line has to tilt down more steeply to still pass through the far points. The two errors compensate for each other.

The cloud on the left is a thousand recordings plotted on module 04's crossplot. It is not a circle. It is a tilted ellipse, leaning the same way as the background trend, and the correlation between the two errors is −0.742.

This has a consequence worth knowing. A quantity measured along the long axis of that ellipse is badly determined, and one measured across it is much better determined than either the intercept or the gradient alone. Distance from the background trend is close to the second kind, which is part of why it is a more stable discriminator than the gradient by itself — though the readouts below show the gain is modest rather than dramatic.

5Can you still tell the rocks apart?

Module 03's answer, retested against the error bars

Module 03 ended by drawing two pairs of rocks and reporting how far apart their curves got: the oil-against-brine pair separated by 0.0256 at thirty degrees and the gas-against-fizz pair by only 0.0123. Those were exact separations between exact curves. Now they can be compared against the error bars you actually get.

The useful measure is the separation divided by its own uncertainty — how many standard deviations apart the two answers are. Below about two, the two clouds overlap enough that a single measurement cannot choose between them.

The result deserves to be stated plainly, because it walks back a claim. At moderate noise the oil and brine sands separate by 1.54 standard deviations in gradient, which is suggestive and not conclusive. At high noise it drops to about half a standard deviation, which is nothing. The fizz pair does not reach one standard deviation even at moderate noise. Module 03 said offset breaks the first tie and not the second. With real error bars the honest version is that offset weakens the first tie and leaves the second where it was.

One consolation: a survey has many traces at many locations, and an anomaly that is consistent over an area is far better evidence than any single gather. That is a real and large effect, and it is why AVO works at all despite everything on this page. It is also why a single-location AVO result, quoted without its spatial context, deserves very little weight.

WWhy it matters

Working with an estimate rather than a measurement

The practical content of this module is that an AVO gradient is an estimate with a large and computable error bar, and that almost nothing in a normal workflow displays that error bar.

  • Compute the error bar; it is free. The uncertainty follows from the angle range, the number of traces and an estimate of the noise. All three are known. There is no excuse for a gradient volume without an uncertainty volume beside it.
  • Argue about the mute. Step 3 shows the mute deciding the quality of the answer. If AVO matters on a project, the mute is an AVO parameter and should be chosen with the AVO in mind rather than inherited from the stacking flow.
  • Do not treat intercept and gradient as independent. Their errors are correlated at −0.742. Anything that combines them — a fluid factor, a projection, a crossplot cut-off — needs the covariance, not two separate error bars.
  • Weight consistency over magnitude. A moderate anomaly that holds together across a mapped area is stronger evidence than a large one at a single location, because the errors this module describes are largely independent from trace to trace and the geology is not.

The failure this module is built against is the confident gradient map. It is a continuous, colourful, plausible-looking volume, and at any single location a substantial part of what it shows is noise. Interpreters know this in the abstract and then pick features off it anyway, because the display gives no clue which features are supported.

The second failure is quieter and it is this site's own. Module 03 reported that adding offset separates the oil sand from the brine sand, and that was true of the exact curves. Step 5 shows that at a realistic noise level the separation is 1.54 standard deviations. Anything computed from noise-free models will overstate what a method can do, and this set of modules is not exempt from that.

The question to carry away is short: how many standard deviations apart are the answers I am trying to choose between? It needs an estimate of the noise, the angle range and the fold, and it can be answered before any data is loaded.

Exercises

  1. Watch the fan open. With the defaults, press record it again ten times and watch where the lines agree and where they disagree.
    HintThe lines all pass close to the same point at the left-hand edge and spread out towards the right. That is the whole of this module in one picture. The fit is pinned where the data is dense in sin²θ — near zero — and has almost nothing holding its slope at the far end. Every result that follows, the eight-and- a-half-times ratio, the lever-arm effect of the mute, the tilted error ellipse, is a restatement of that fan.
  2. Prove the ratio is geometry, not data quality. Read the intercept and gradient uncertainties in step 2 at moderate noise, then switch to noisy and read them again.
    HintAt moderate noise the uncertainties are 0.0056 and 0.0466, a ratio of 8.3. At noisy they are 0.0149 and 0.1244 — both about 2.7 times larger, and the ratio is 8.3 again. Doubling the noise doubles both and changes nothing about their relationship. The ratio depends only on how the angles are distributed, so it can be worked out from the acquisition geometry alone, before anyone looks at the data.
  3. Cost out the mute. In step 3, read the gradient uncertainty at 40°, then at 30°, then at 20°, then at 15°.
    HintWith moderate noise and 16 traces the gradient uncertainty runs 0.0280 at 40°, 0.0466 at 30°, 0.1002 at 20° and 0.1753 at 15° — a factor of 6.3 across that span. Meanwhile the intercept barely moves. A mute decision taken to tidy up a stack can quietly cost most of the information in the gradient, and nothing downstream will announce it.
  4. Find out what more traces are worth. In step 3, raise the trace count from 16 to 48 and read the gradient uncertainty at each.
    HintSixteen traces give 0.0466 and forty-eight give 0.0281, a factor of 1.66 for three times the data. Theory says √3 = 1.73; the small shortfall is because adding traces also changes how the angles are distributed, not just how many there are. Compare that with exercise 3, where going from a 20° mute to a 40° mute improved the gradient by a factor of 3.6 at no cost in acquisition at all. Angle range is by far the cheaper lever.
  5. Retest module 03's conclusion. In step 5, read how many standard deviations separate each pair at moderate noise, then at noisy.
    HintAt moderate noise the oil and brine sands are 1.54 standard deviations apart in gradient and the gas and fizz sands 0.81. At high noise they fall to 0.58 and 0.31. Widening the mute to 35° would bring the oil pair to two sigma; nothing brings the fizz pair there. Two standard deviations is a weak threshold and neither pair reaches it. Module 03's exact curves said the first tie was broken and the second was not; with error bars, the first is weakened and the second is untouched. The conclusion is not wrong, but it was stated more strongly than noise-free modelling can support.

Key points

  • A fit is anchored at the near offsets and loose at the far ones. Repeated recordings of the same rock give lines that converge at zero and fan apart with angle. Everything else in this module follows from that.
  • The gradient carries almost all the uncertainty. At a 0–30° fit its scatter is 8.3 times the intercept's, and that ratio is set by the angle range alone — doubling the noise doubles both and leaves the ratio unchanged.
  • The mute is an AVO parameter. Gradient uncertainty runs 0.0280 at a 40° fit and 0.1753 at 15°, a factor of 6.3, with the rock and the per-trace noise identical throughout.
  • Fold pays square-root returns and angle range pays linear ones. Tripling the traces improves the gradient by 1.66; going from a 20° to a 40° mute improves it by 3.6 and costs nothing to acquire.
  • The two errors are correlated at −0.742. The cloud of recovered values is a tilted ellipse, so quoting separate error bars on intercept and gradient claims more than the data supports.
  • With real error bars, module 03's separations are weaker than they looked. The oil and brine sands are 1.54 standard deviations apart at moderate noise and 0.58 at high noise; the gas and fizz sands never reach one. Noise-free modelling overstates what any method can do.

MMethod, simplifications and references

What is computed, and where it departs from production software

The rock physics and the reflection coefficients are modules 01 and 03's, unchanged. What this module adds is noise and a fit.

The uncertainties are exact, not simulated. For a least-squares straight line through points with independent noise of standard deviation σ, the uncertainty in the slope is σ/√Sxx and in the intercept σ√(1/n + x̄²/Sxx), where Sxx is the spread of the sin²θ values about their own mean. Those formulas are what the readouts use. The scatter clouds and histograms are simulated, and the test suite checks that thirty thousand simulated fits reproduce the formulas' widths to within about half a percent, and their correlation to within 0.02.

The noise model is deliberately simple. Independent, identically distributed Gaussian noise is added to each trace's picked amplitude, at 0.015 or 0.040 in reflection-coefficient units. Real gather noise is none of those things: it is correlated between neighbouring traces, usually worse at far offsets than near, and often not Gaussian at all. Correlated noise is the important omission, because noise that varies smoothly with offset looks exactly like a gradient and this model cannot produce that failure.

Where this departs from production practice.

  • Amplitudes are picked perfectly. The pick is taken at the true time on every trace. Real picking has to contend with residual moveout, and a small timing error at far offset turns directly into an amplitude error that is systematic rather than random.
  • No NMO stretch. Far-offset wavelets are stretched to lower frequency by the moveout correction, which changes the amplitude of a tuned event with offset for reasons that have nothing to do with the rocks. On a thin bed this can dominate everything shown here.
  • Noise is the same at every angle. In practice fold falls off at far offset, so the far traces — the ones the gradient depends on most — are usually the noisiest.
  • The fit is unweighted and the angles are exact. Production fits are often weighted, and are usually done against straight-ray angles, which module 03 showed to be biased.
  • Nothing here is spatial. The single largest defence against everything in this module is that anomalies repeat over an area while noise does not, and a single-trace analysis cannot show that.
