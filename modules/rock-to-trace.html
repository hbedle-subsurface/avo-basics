Module 01  ·  Rock physics to seismic

Build a rock, make a seismic trace

A seismic amplitude is the far end of a chain. Grains and pores set a stiffness, the fluid in the pores changes it, stiffness and density make an impedance, the contrast between two impedances makes a reflection, a wavelet smears that reflection out in time, and noise sits on top of the result. Every link in that chain throws something away. Build the rock at the top of this page and follow what happens to it.

1Grains, pores, and whatever is in the pores

Three ingredients, two of which you control

A clastic reservoir is a pile of mineral grains with space between them. Three numbers describe it for our purposes: what the grains are made of, how much space is left over, and what is sitting in that space. Everything else in this module follows from those three.

Drag porosity and watch the grains separate. Drag clay content and watch the grain color shift as quartz is replaced by something softer. Switch the fluid and watch the pore space change color. At partial saturation the gas is drawn as fine speckle rather than a layer on top, because that is the arrangement the physics further down this page assumes.

Density is the simple case. The bulk density of the rock is the volume-weighted average of the grains and the fluid, and nothing subtle happens to it. Swap brine for gas and the density drops by an amount you can work out on paper. Stiffness is not like that at all, which is the subject of the next step.

2From the rock to Vp, Vs and density

The fluid changes the stiffness, but not the way you would guess

The rock's stiffness comes in two pieces. The dry frame is what the grain pack would do with nothing in the pores: it stiffens as porosity falls and the grains press harder on each other. That is the left panel, and the fluid has no say in it at all.

Then the pore fluid is put back. It resists being squeezed, so it stiffens the rock against compression — and only against compression. It does nothing whatever against shear, because a fluid cannot support a shear stress. That asymmetry is what makes fluid detection from seismic possible at all. This calculation is called Gassmann's equation, and you have now seen what it does before meeting it as an equation.

Now put gas into a brine-filled rock and drag the saturation up from zero. The curve does not slope. It falls off a cliff in the first few percent and then flattens out, which means a rock with a trace of gas in it looks very nearly like a rock full of gas. Interpreters call the trap that follows from this fizz gas.

Watch Vs while you add gas. It goes up. The shear modulus has not moved — Gassmann leaves it alone — but the rock got lighter, and Vs is the square root of stiffness over density, so losing mass with the same shear stiffness speeds the shear wave up. Vp meanwhile falls, because it lost far more stiffness than mass. Vp down and Vs up at the same time is a much stronger fluid signature than either one alone, Vp/Vs and AVO both rest on it.

3Impedance, and the first thing you lose

Two numbers into one, then only the contrast survives

Acoustic impedance is velocity times density. That is the whole definition, and it is also the first place information goes missing, because it is a product. A fast light rock and a slow heavy rock can have exactly the same impedance. Once you are looking at impedance you can no longer tell them apart, and seismic never sees anything but impedance.

The second loss follows immediately. A reflection coefficient is the contrast between two impedances, normalized: R = (I₂ − I₁) / (I₂ + I₁). Double both layers and R does not move. So the reflection tells you about the step, and nothing at all about the level. A big reflection means the two rocks are different, not that either one is unusual.

This is why there is a shale velocity slider on this page. Leave the reservoir completely alone and change only the shale, and the reflection changes anyway — it can even change sign. The anomaly you are looking at belongs to the pair of rocks, not to the reservoir.

The top and base coefficients here are equal and opposite, because the rock above and the rock below the reservoir are the same shale. Real reservoirs rarely do, and when the underlying unit differs the base reflection carries its own separate information. Keeping them symmetric for now makes the wavelet interference in the next step much easier to read.

4The wavelet, and what it smears out

Two spikes go in; something less specific comes out

The earth gives you the two spikes on the left. The seismic experiment does not record spikes — it records a band-limited wavelet, once for each spike, added together. That convolution is the third place information is lost, and unlike the first two it is a loss of resolution in time rather than in rock properties.

Take the reservoir thickness down. Above a certain thickness the top and base arrive as two separate events you can pick. Below it they overlap, they stop being separable, and the amplitude you measure at the top is no longer the top's reflection alone — it has the base's reflection mixed into it. The thickness where the two wavelets line up constructively is called tuning.

The textbook rule puts tuning at a quarter of the wavelength. This page does not quote that rule, it measures the thickness where the amplitude actually peaks, and the two do not agree: the measured value comes out consistently thinner. That is not a bug in either. A quarter wavelength is where a top and base of the same sign tune. Here they have opposite signs, and an opposite-signed pair tunes when the two wavelets are separated by the wavelet's own peak-to-trough time, which for a Ricker is √6/2πf — about 0.39/f rather than 0.5/f. Both numbers are printed below so you can watch the gap.

Then leave the rock completely alone and move the frequency slider. Nothing about the subsurface has changed. The trace changes anyway. That is the cleanest possible statement of what seismic data is: an observation of the model, not the model.

The measured amplitude and the reflection coefficient are printed side by side so you can compare them. Away from tuning they agree to two decimal places; at tuning the measured amplitude runs about 45% above the reflection coefficient, so a thin dull reservoir can look identical to a thick bright one. This is a resolution question rather than a fluid question, and it is treated properly in the companion seismic resolution modules. It appears here only so you know it is one of the things standing between the rock and the amplitude.

5Noise, and what is left to conclude

The measurement has an error bar, and things hide inside it

Everything so far has been exact. Now add the last link. The panel on the left is the same trace repeated with different noise realizations at the level set in the header — same rock, same wavelet, a different noise realization each time. Turn the noise up and watch how much of the amplitude you would report is actually the noise.

The panel on the right draws two different rocks: an oil sand at 30% porosity, and a brine sand at 35% porosity with no hydrocarbon in it at all. Read the two reflection coefficients. Then read the noise. Then decide whether you could tell these apart from a trace.

That is what the chain does. Grain size and sorting and cement and clay all collapse into one dry frame; the fluid enters only through a single bulk modulus; velocity and density collapse into one impedance; two impedances collapse into one contrast; the wavelet blurs the contrast in time; and noise puts an error bar around what is left. Six numbers went in and one came out. Running that backwards from the one number is the job, and it does not have a unique answer.

Those two rocks do differ in Vp/Vs, by a wide margin, and that is the thread module 03 picks up. A single normal-incidence amplitude cannot see Vp/Vs, because at normal incidence no shear is involved. Tilt the ray and the shear properties start to contribute, and one measurement becomes two. It does not remove the ambiguity, but it does reduce it.

WWhy it matters

Where this chain is used in real work

Running rock properties forward to a synthetic trace is not an academic exercise. It is the routine that sits underneath most quantitative interpretation.

  • Well ties. A synthetic built from the sonic and density logs is compared with the seismic at the well. Where they disagree, something in the chain is wrong, and knowing the chain is how you work out which link.
  • Feasibility studies. Before anyone shoots or reprocesses anything, the question is whether the fluid you are hoping for would even be visible in this rock at this depth. You answer it by building the rock and looking at the predicted contrast, which is what this page does.
  • Fluid substitution on logs. A well found brine. Would the same sand have shown an anomaly if it had held gas? Gassmann answers that, and the answer decides whether a nearby amplitude justifies a well.
  • Forward modeling for interpretation. Rather than inverting the seismic, propose several geological models, push each one through the chain, and see which ones can produce the observed response. More than one model usually survives.

It misleads in two ways. The first is that a forward model can convince you of more than it has shown. You built a gas sand, the synthetic went bright, and the real data is bright, so the reservoir contains gas — except that you never checked what else in your parameter space also goes bright. The model demonstrated that gas is sufficient to explain the amplitude. Sufficiency is not the same as necessity, and it is not evidence for gas until you have shown that the plausible alternatives fail.

There is a second problem. Every number in the chain has an uncertainty, and some of them are large. The clay bulk modulus used here is a value on which the published literature genuinely disagrees. Effective pressure comes from an assumed overburden. Saturation is barely constrained by amplitude at all, as step 2 showed. A synthetic trace is drawn as a single line, which hides all of that. The line should be a band, and the band is often wide enough to contain several different geological stories.

Run the chain more than once. Change the thing you are least sure about — the shale, the porosity, the saturation, the clay — and see whether your conclusion survives. If one run of the model is the whole argument, the argument is weaker than it looks.

Exercises

  1. Find the porosity where a brine sand disappears. Set the fluid to brine, clay to 0%, shale velocity to 2700 m/s, and walk porosity from 5% up to 35%, reading R at the top in step 3 at each 5% stop. Where does the reflection change sign, and what does the trace look like there?
    HintR at the top runs +0.331 at 5% porosity, +0.244 at 10%, +0.170 at 15%, +0.105 at 20%, +0.045 at 25%, then −0.011 at 30% and −0.064 at 35%. It crosses zero at about 29% porosity. At that point the sand and the shale have the same impedance and the trace is flat: there is a reservoir there, and seismic cannot see it. Note also that at 5% porosity the brine sand is a very strong positive reflection. A bright event is not evidence of anything by itself — a tight sand is bright too.
  2. Make a gas sand invisible. Now set the fluid to gas at 100% saturation and put porosity at 20%. Read R at the top. Then raise porosity to 30% and read it again.
    HintAt 20% porosity the gas sand gives R = −0.004, which is nothing at all — the trace is essentially flat over the reservoir. At 30% porosity the same gas gives R = −0.162, an unmistakable bright spot. The gas did not change. What changed is the rock it is sitting in, and therefore how far the gas moves it relative to the shale. "Gas gives a bright spot" is false as stated; it is the combination of gas and a rock soft enough for the gas to matter, against a shale it can get below.
  3. Watch the fizz-gas trap happen. Porosity 30%, fluid gas. Move saturation from 0% up in small steps and read Vp and R after each move. How much gas do you need before you have most of the anomaly?
    HintR goes −0.011 at 0% gas, −0.063 at 2%, −0.091 at 5%, −0.110 at 10%, −0.125 at 20%, and −0.162 at 100%. Two percent gas has already produced 35% of the entire amplitude change; ten percent has produced 66%; twenty percent has produced 76%. The whole span from 20% to 100% saturation moves the amplitude less than the first two percent does. This is why amplitude alone cannot distinguish a commercial gas column from a residual gas or fizz-gas zone, and why that distinction has been drilled wrong many times.
  4. Change nothing about the reservoir and change the answer anyway. Set a gas sand at 30% porosity and note R at the top. Leave every reservoir control alone. Now move the shale velocity from 2300 m/s to 3100 m/s and read R again at each end.
    HintWith shale at 2300 m/s the top reflection is −0.0626; at 2700 m/s it is −0.1615; at 3100 m/s it is −0.2442. The reservoir is identical in every respect. The reflection coefficient grew by a factor of 3.9 because R measures the contrast between two rocks, and you changed the other one. Read this together with exercise 2: the same gas sand can be invisible or spectacular depending entirely on what it is sitting under. An amplitude map is as much a map of the seal as of the reservoir.
  5. Two rocks, one amplitude. Set an oil sand, 30% porosity, 100% saturation, API 32, GOR 0. Record R. Then switch to brine and set porosity to 35%, leaving everything else alone. Compare, and then look at Vp/Vs for each.
    HintThe oil sand gives R = −0.0637. The brine sand gives R = −0.0636. They agree to four decimal places, which is far inside any noise level you can set on this page — step 5 draws both traces so you can see that they are the same picture. One has oil in it and one does not. Their Vp/Vs values, however, are 1.71 and 1.92, which is a large difference. Normal-incidence amplitude is blind to it because no shear is involved at normal incidence. Module 03 tilts the ray, which is how that difference is recovered.

Key points

  • Density is an average; stiffness is not. Bulk density is the volume-weighted mean of grains and fluid and nothing surprising happens to it. The bulk modulus is governed by Gassmann, where a pore fluid stiffens the rock against compression and does nothing against shear. That asymmetry is the whole basis of fluid detection.
  • Gas does not act in proportion to how much of it there is. The pore fluid modulus is a harmonic average, so the softest component dominates. Two percent gas gives about a third of the full amplitude effect and ten percent gives two thirds. Amplitude cannot tell a producible column from a residual one.
  • Impedance is a product, so it loses information. Velocity and density go in, one number comes out, and a fast light rock and a slow heavy rock can land on the same value. Seismic never sees anything but impedance.
  • A reflection is a contrast, not a level. R depends on both rocks equally. Changing the shale alone can quadruple the reservoir's reflection or flip its sign. An amplitude anomaly is a statement about a boundary, and the reservoir owns only half of it.
  • The wavelet blurs the answer in time. Below about a quarter wavelength the top and base reflections interfere and the amplitude you measure includes both. A thin bright reservoir and a thick dull one can produce the same peak.
  • Different rocks can produce identical traces. An oil sand at 30% porosity and a plain brine sand at 35% differ in R by 0.0001 in this model. Their Vp/Vs differ by 0.2. A single normal-incidence amplitude cannot separate them, which is the problem the rest of this site is about.

MMethod, simplifications and references

What is computed, and where it departs from production software

How it is computed. Every panel is generated live from the controls in the header; there are no stored images and no curves drawn to look plausible. The chain is: mineral mix by Voigt-Reuss-Hill, dry frame by the Dvorkin-Nur soft-sand model, pore fluid by Batzle-Wang, fluid mixing by the Reuss average, saturated bulk modulus by Gassmann, then Vp and Vs from the moduli and density, impedance, reflection coefficients, convolution with a Ricker wavelet, and band-limited additive noise.

The dry frame. Hertz-Mindlin contact theory at a critical porosity of 40%, joined to the mineral point by the modified Hashin-Shtrikman lower bound, with a coordination number of 9 and an effective pressure of 23 MPa. This is the friable-sand or soft-sand model, appropriate to young unconsolidated clastics — which is where bright spots and AVO are actually used. It is not appropriate to a cemented or carbonate reservoir, which would need a contact-cement or stiff-sand model and would give considerably faster rocks at the same porosity.

The obvious alternative is Nur's critical-porosity model, Kdry = Kmin(1 − φ/φc), which is far simpler and is what many courses use. It is an upper bound, and at 20% porosity it gives a brine sand of 4679 m/s against the soft-sand model's 3202 m/s — a reflection coefficient of +0.287 instead of +0.105. That is a larger effect than the fluid has. The choice of dry-frame model changes the answer by more than the fluid does, and any course that presents one model without saying so is hiding the largest uncertainty in the calculation.

Where this departs from production software.

  • Effective pressure is fixed at 23 MPa, corresponding to a 2000 m burial depth with a hydrostatic pore pressure. Real work varies it with depth and with overpressure, and overpressure softens rocks in a way that mimics gas.
  • The overburden traveltime is fixed at 1.500 s regardless of the shale velocity slider, so that the time axis does not slide underneath you when you change the shale. In reality the whole column above would shift.
  • Clay is treated as part of the load-bearing frame, mixed into the mineral by Voigt-Reuss-Hill. Dispersed clay sitting in the pores behaves quite differently, and real shaly sands need a model that distinguishes the two. The clay slider here is honest only over the small range it offers.
  • The bounding shale is empirical, not modeled: Castagna's mudrock line for Vs and Gardner's relation for density, both driven from the single velocity slider. Shales are not grain packs and a contact model does not apply to them. These are regressions, and an individual shale can sit well off either trend.
  • Fluid mixing is uniform (Reuss), which is Gassmann's own assumption. Patchy saturation is stiffer and can give a noticeably weaker anomaly at the same saturation. The gap between the two is a real uncertainty that this module does not yet show.
  • Gassmann assumes the shear modulus is unaffected by the fluid, that the rock is homogeneous and isotropic, that the pores are connected, and that the measurement is at low enough frequency for pore pressure to equilibrate. Seismic-frequency data mostly satisfies the last one; log-frequency data often does not, which is why sonic logs and seismic disagree about fluid effects.
  • No anisotropy, no attenuation, no multiples, no transmission losses, no geometric spreading, and normal incidence only. Angle-dependence is module 03.
  • Noise is band-limited, generated as white reflectivity convolved with the same wavelet, so it looks like seismic. White noise would look like static and would be far too easy to see past. Its amplitude is fixed in reflection-coefficient units rather than scaled to the signal, so that a weak reflector genuinely can be lost in it.
  • The two shales are identical, above and below, which makes the base reflection exactly the negative of the top. Real sections seldom are.

Verification. The physics is checked against closed forms rather than against itself: Batzle-Wang water velocity against the published value at 20 °C, Gassmann returning the dry frame when the fluid modulus goes to zero, a brine-to-gas-and-back substitution round trip landing within 10⁻¹⁵ GPa of its start, exact Zoeppritz at zero degrees equalling (I₂−I₁)/(I₂+I₁) to machine precision, and both Zoeppritz and Shuey returning exactly zero across an interface with no contrast. Every number quoted in the exercises above is read out of this running page rather than estimated.
