Module 03  ·  The rock

From a rock to a trace

Modules 01 and 02 built a rock: grains, pore space, a fluid, and the four properties that come out — Vp, Vs, density and the two moduli behind them. None of that is a seismic measurement yet. This module follows the rock the rest of the way to a trace and keeps track of what is lost at each step, because two of the four properties do not arrive and the two that do arrive together.

The reservoir, the shale around it, and the trace they make — every panel on the page is computed from these five controls

Five steps, each one a place where something about the rock stops travelling with the signal. Every number quoted in the text is read off the running page.

1A reflection needs two rocks

The reservoir on its own reflects nothing

Everything in modules 01 and 02 was about a single rock. A reflection is not a property of a rock. It happens where one rock meets a different one, and it depends on both of them equally.

So a reservoir needs a neighbour before there is anything to measure. Here it is a shale, above and below, and the shale has its own velocity, its own density and its own shear velocity — none of which the reservoir knows anything about. Its P-wave velocity is the fourth control on this page, and it will turn out to matter as much as the porosity does.

The two rocks are built differently. The reservoir comes from the grain-pack model of module 01 with a fluid inserted by module 02. The shale comes from an empirical trend, because a contact model between spherical grains does not describe a shale at all. That difference is worth knowing about: the two rocks on this page are not equally well founded.

Six numbers describe this pair completely: a Vp, a Vs and a density for each rock. The rest of this module is about how few of them reach the trace.

2Two numbers become one

The first loss

A normal-incidence reflection responds to the acoustic impedance of each rock, which is its P-wave velocity multiplied by its density. Two numbers go in and one comes out, and that is a loss, because a product cannot be undone. If two numbers multiply to twelve they might have been three and four, or two and six, or one and a half and eight.

The right-hand panel makes that concrete. It is the plane of all possible velocities and densities, with a curve drawn through every combination that has the same impedance as the reservoir. Every rock on that curve is a different rock, and no normal-incidence measurement distinguishes any of them from any other. A fast light rock and a slow heavy one land on the same point as far as the seismic is concerned.

This is the first of three places where the chain from a rock to an amplitude throws something away. It is worth counting them as they go past, because the count is what makes working backwards from an amplitude hard.

The shear velocity has not been lost yet — it simply has not been used. Nothing in a normal-incidence impedance contains it, which is step 5.

3Only the contrast survives

The second loss

The reflection coefficient at the top of the reservoir is the difference in impedance across the boundary divided by the sum: R = (I₂ − I₁) / (I₂ + I₁). Two impedances go in and one number comes out, and again something is gone. Double both impedances and R does not move at all. A reflection reports the step and says nothing about the level.

The consequence is easy to miss and it matters more than almost anything else on this page. The amplitude belongs to the pair of rocks, not to the reservoir. Leave the reservoir completely alone — same porosity, same fluid, same everything — and change only the shale velocity. The reflection changes anyway, and within the range of this one control it passes through zero and changes sign.

The right-hand panel plots that directly: the reflection coefficient at the top of the reservoir as the shale velocity runs across its range, with the reservoir held fixed. The crossing point is where the two rocks happen to have the same impedance and the reflection vanishes entirely — a real reservoir, in a real shale, invisible.

A bright amplitude therefore means the two rocks are different. It does not mean the reservoir is unusual, and an interpretation that treats the anomaly as a property of the sand alone has already made a mistake that no later processing recovers from.

The top and base coefficients are equal and opposite here because the rock above and below the reservoir is the same shale. Real reservoirs rarely sit in a symmetric section, and when the unit below differs the base reflection carries information of its own.

4The wavelet, and the trace

The third loss, covered properly elsewhere

The earth supplies two reflection coefficients, one at each boundary, at two instants in time. A seismic experiment does not record instants. It records a band-limited pulse, once for each reflection, added together — which is the convolution the resolution modules build from scratch.

The step is included here to close the chain rather than to teach it. The frequency control moves the wavelet without touching the rock, and the trace changes anyway. That is the cleanest available statement of what a seismic trace is: an observation of the model rather than the model.

The reservoir is held thick enough that its top and base stay separate at every frequency the control reaches, so nothing on this page tunes. What happens when a bed is too thin for that — and it is a great deal — belongs to the wedge module in the resolution set.

5What never arrived at all

The shear velocity is not in the answer

Modules 01 and 02 spent most of their length on the shear response: where it comes from, why a pore fluid leaves it alone, and why that makes a fluid visible. None of it has been used on this page. A normal-incidence reflection coefficient is built from Vp and density and nothing else. Vs appears nowhere in it.

That is not an approximation. At normal incidence the rock is compressed and nothing slides, so there is no shear deformation for the shear modulus to resist, and the shear velocity cannot enter the answer. Two rocks with the same impedance and completely different Vp/Vs produce identical traces.

The left-hand panel finds those rocks. It searches porosity and gas saturation for every combination that gives the reservoir's impedance, and there are many: the same impedance is reached by a high-porosity rock with a little gas and a lower-porosity rock full of it. The right-hand panel shows what those rocks have that the trace does not — a range of Vp/Vs values, all producing the same amplitude.

At the settings this module opens with the search finds sixteen such rocks, running from 2% gas saturation to 100%. Their reflection coefficients span 0.004 and their Vp/Vs values span 1.47 to 1.68 — a difference of about 14% in a rock property, arriving at the measurement as nothing at all.

Two things follow. The saturation ambiguity from module 02 survives into the amplitude, so a normal-incidence bright spot indicates gas without indicating how much. And a property that would separate these rocks exists in them and is not being measured. Getting at it is what the rest of this set does, and it starts with letting the wave arrive at an angle.

WWhy it matters

Where this chain is used, and where it misleads

Modelling a prospect before drilling. The forward chain on this page is what a synthetic seismogram is. Build the rock, pair it with its neighbour, take the contrast, apply the wavelet, and compare the result with the real data. Every amplitude study starts here.

Knowing what the shale is doing. Step 3 is the reason a mapped amplitude anomaly can follow a change in the overburden rather than a change in the reservoir. Where the sealing shale varies laterally, the amplitude varies with it, and the resulting map looks like a fluid effect.

Reading an impedance inversion. An inversion recovers impedance, which is further along the chain than velocity and density but still short of the rock. The velocity-density ambiguity in step 2 does not go away because the data were inverted.

Knowing when to ask for more. Step 5 is a statement about what a single stacked amplitude can and cannot contain. When a question depends on Vp/Vs — which fluid questions usually do — a normal-incidence amplitude is not capable of answering it, however good the data are.

Exercises

Each answer is a number read off this page at the settings described.

  1. Change the amplitude without touching the reservoir. Leave the reservoir at 30% porosity, 100% gas and no clay. Move only the shale velocity from 2000 to 3400 m/s and watch the reflection coefficient at the top.
    AnswerIt goes from +0.025 at 2000 m/s to −0.298 at 3400 — through zero, changing sign, and ending at a strong negative reflection — with the reservoir identical throughout. The reflection is a property of the pair. An amplitude map that brightens across a survey is as likely to be telling you about the shale as about the sand, and nothing in the amplitude itself says which.
  2. Make the reservoir disappear. With the same reservoir, find the shale velocity at which the reflection coefficient reaches zero.
    AnswerAround 2081 m/s, where the shale happens to have the same impedance as the gas sand. At that point a fully gas-charged reservoir produces no reflection at all. It is still there, it still holds gas, and the seismic has nothing to show. This is not a rare accident: impedance is a product of two numbers that both vary, and the values coincide somewhere.
  3. Confirm that only the contrast matters. Compare the reflection coefficient with the one the page reports for both impedances doubled.
    AnswerThey are identical to every digit shown. Doubling both impedances doubles the numerator and the denominator of (I₂ − I₁)/(I₂ + I₁), so nothing changes. The reflection carries the step and discards the level entirely, which is why an amplitude alone cannot tell you whether you are looking at two soft rocks or two hard ones.
  4. Move the seismic without moving the earth. Fix every rock control and drag the wavelet frequency from 15 to 60 Hz, watching the trace and the measured peak.
    AnswerThe trace changes shape substantially — the events narrow as the frequency rises — while the reflection coefficients underneath are untouched. The measured peak stays close to the reflection coefficient because the reservoir is thick enough to stay resolved at every frequency here. What you are looking at is the earth convolved with the experiment, and separating the two is most of what seismic processing is for.
  5. Find the rocks the trace cannot separate. Look at step 5 with the reservoir at 30% porosity and 100% gas, and read the saturation range of the equal-impedance family.
    AnswerSixteen rocks are found, running from 2% gas saturation up to 100%, across porosities from 30.0% to 37.5%. Every one of them has the reservoir's impedance to within half a percent, so their reflection coefficients span only 0.004 and their traces are indistinguishable. Their Vp/Vs values are not indistinguishable at all: they run from 1.472 to 1.684, a spread of about 14%. That is a large difference sitting in the rocks and reaching none of the measurement. The fizz-gas ambiguity from module 02 has survived intact all the way to the amplitude.

Key points

  • A reflection is a property of two rocks. A reservoir on its own reflects nothing, and the rock it sits in matters as much as the rock itself.
  • Six numbers describe the pair — a Vp, a Vs and a density each — and a normal-incidence amplitude is one number. Most of the description does not survive the trip.
  • Impedance is a product, so velocity and density collapse into one number and cannot be separated again. A fast light rock and a slow heavy one are the same rock to the seismic.
  • A reflection coefficient is a ratio, so only the contrast survives and the level is discarded. Double both impedances and the reflection is unchanged.
  • So changing the shale alone changes the anomaly, and can remove it entirely. There is a shale velocity at which a fully gas-charged reservoir gives no reflection at all.
  • The wavelet is the third loss, and it acts in time rather than on the rock properties. Moving the frequency changes the trace while the earth stands still.
  • The shear velocity never enters. At normal incidence nothing slides, so Vs cannot appear in the answer, and rocks with the same impedance and different Vp/Vs give identical traces.
  • The fizz-gas ambiguity survives to the amplitude. Saturations from 2% to 100% share this reservoir's impedance to within half a percent, so a bright spot indicates gas without indicating how much.

MMethod, simplifications and references

What is computed, and what is fixed

The reservoir is the module 01 soft-sand frame with the module 02 fluid put in by Gassmann's relation, at a fixed 23 MPa and 64 °C. Pressure and temperature are not controls here because this module is about what happens after the rock exists; module 02 is where they belong.

The shale is Castagna's mudrock line for Vs and Gardner's relation for density, both driven from the single velocity control. These are empirical trends fitted to data rather than models of a shale, and they are used because a contact model between spherical grains does not describe a shale at all. The two rocks on this page therefore rest on quite different footings, and the shale's Vs in particular should be treated as a plausible value rather than a computed one.

The reflection coefficients are the normal-incidence formula, (I₂ − I₁)/(I₂ + I₁), with impedance as Vp times density. This is exact at zero incidence angle and is the only case this module covers. Everything from module 04 onward is about what happens when the angle is not zero.

The equal-impedance search in step 5 scans porosity from 15% to 38% and gas saturation from 0 to 100%, keeping any rock whose impedance is within 0.5% of the reservoir's. The tolerance is there because the search is on a grid; a finer grid would fill in the curve rather than change it. The family it finds is real, but it is narrow: module 07 runs the same idea across clay content, the shale and the wavelet as well, and finds far more.

Fixed rather than controlled:

  • The reservoir is 60 m thick and stays thick enough to be resolved at every frequency the control reaches, so nothing on this page tunes. Tuning is the subject of the resolution modules and it is a large effect that this module deliberately steps around.
  • The section is symmetric — the same shale above and below — which makes the two reflections equal and opposite and the trace easy to read. Real sections are rarely symmetric.
  • No noise. Every trace here is exact. What noise does to a measured amplitude is module 08.
  • Normal incidence only, so no offset, no angle and no shear.
