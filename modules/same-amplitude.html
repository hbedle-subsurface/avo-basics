Module 02  ·  Rock physics to seismic

Same bright spot, different rock

Module 01 ran the calculation forwards, from a rock to an amplitude, and that direction is exact. This module runs it the other way. Here is an amplitude somebody measured. Your job is to say what rock produced it — and the honest answer is that a great many rocks did. This page counts them.

1An amplitude, and no rock attached to it

One number is all you have

The trace on the left is the measurement. It is the only thing you are given. There is no well, no log, and nobody to tell you what is down there. It shows a strong trough at the top of something, which is what an interpreter would call a bright spot.

The controls above build a candidate rock, and the trace on the right is what that rock would produce. Move the sliders until the two traces agree. The bar underneath shows how close you are, with the tolerance set by the noise on the measurement.

Then keep going. Having found one match, go and find another that is a genuinely different rock — different porosity, different fluid, different saturation. The Show me another match button does it for you, and it will keep doing it, because there are thousands. That is the entire subject of this module.

Notice what the match does not require. Nothing checks whether your rock is geologically sensible, whether that porosity occurs at this depth, or whether a gas column that thick could be trapped. The seismic amplitude cannot see any of that. It sees one number, and every rock that produces that number is, as far as the seismic is concerned, equally good.

2Porosity against gas saturation

The trade-off that fizz gas hides inside

Take the first two unknowns together. The picture on the left is worth a moment before you read anything into it, because the same kind of picture is used in the next two steps as well.

How to read it. It is not a map of the ground. Every single point in it is a different rock: its position across tells you how much gas that rock holds, its position up the side tells you its porosity, and its colour tells you what seismic amplitude it would produce — blue for a peak, red for a trough, white for nothing at all. Somewhere in that field are the rocks that produce your amplitude, and the black line runs through all of them. The small circle is the rock currently on your sliders.

So the black line is the answer to "what could this be", drawn all at once. Every point on it fits the measurement exactly as well as every other point. The panel beside it takes three rocks off that line and writes them out in full, with the trace each one produces, so you can see that they really are indistinguishable.

Read the three cards. A rock at 30% porosity full of gas, and a rock at 34% porosity with a tenth as much gas in it, sit on the same line and give the same trace. One is a discovery. The other is a wet sand with a trace of residual gas, and drilling it would be a dry hole.

The line is nearly flat over most of its length, which is the fizz-gas result from module 01 seen from the other side. Because the first few percent of gas does most of the work, almost the entire saturation axis maps into a narrow band of amplitude, and the contour has to run almost horizontally to stay inside it.

3The rock you did not measure

Half the reflection belongs to the shale

A reflection coefficient is a contrast between two rocks, so half of every amplitude belongs to the unit above. In module 01 you saw that changing the shale alone moved the reflection by a factor of nearly four. Here it works in reverse: because the shale is not known exactly, a whole range of reservoirs becomes admissible.

Same picture as before, with the shale velocity along the bottom this time instead of saturation. Every point is still one rock, the colour is still the amplitude it would produce, and the black line still runs through the ones that match your measurement. Follow that line: a slow shale demands a soft, porous reservoir to produce the contrast, and a fast shale is satisfied by a much tighter one. The porosity you would report depends on a property of a rock you are not even interested in. The two cards beside it are the rocks at each end of that line.

This is the argument for using an interval that brackets the seal in any inversion, and the reason a nearby well that penetrated only the overburden is still worth a great deal. It constrains the half of the reflection that has nothing to do with the reservoir.

4Thin and bright, or thick and dull

Tuning adds a third way to reach the same number

The picture on the left works exactly as it did in the last two steps: every point is a rock, now at a different thickness as well as a different porosity, coloured by the amplitude it would give, with the black line through the ones that match.

Module 01 showed that below about a quarter wavelength the top and base reflections interfere and the measured amplitude runs above the reflection coefficient — by up to 45% for this wavelet. Running that backwards, a thin reservoir needs a weaker reflection coefficient to reach the same measured amplitude, and a weaker reflection coefficient means a stiffer, tighter rock.

So thickness and reservoir quality trade off against each other, and the trade is asymmetric in a way that matters. Below tuning, a thinner reservoir needs a tighter rock, so the measurement does set a lower bound on thickness. Above tuning the factor is exactly one and stays there, so every greater thickness produces an identical amplitude. The measurement puts no upper bound on thickness whatsoever. The two traces on the right are the thinnest and the thickest reservoirs on this panel that fit, and the second of those is only the end of the slider.

This is the trap behind amplitude-derived volumetrics. A bright amplitude gets read as a good reservoir, when part of the brightness was thickness all along — and the part that was thickness pushes the estimate in the opposite direction from the part that was quality. Below tuning, brighter means thinner.

5Count them all

The whole solution set, and what shrinks it

The three previous steps each held everything but two parameters fixed. Now let all of them vary at once. WWhy it matters

What to do about a problem that has no unique answer

Non-uniqueness is not an argument against using amplitudes. It is an argument for using them a particular way, and the difference shows up in how a prospect is described.

  • Report a range, not a value. An inversion returns one number per sample because it was asked for one. The set of models consistent with the data is what step 5 draws, and a porosity quoted without its admissible range has thrown away the part a decision depends on.
  • Spend money where it narrows the set. Step 5 puts a number on what each piece of outside information is worth. Knowing the shale removes nine tenths of the solutions here. Nothing you can learn about the other parameters narrows the saturation, because the amplitude never carried that information in the first place.
  • Test the alternatives, not the favourite. The useful question is not whether your model fits. It is which other models also fit, and whether any of them would change the decision. A model that fits is evidence only in proportion to how many rivals it beat.
  • Be specific about which ambiguity you have. "The amplitude is ambiguous" is not actionable. "This amplitude is consistent with 30% porosity at full gas saturation and with 34% porosity at 10% saturation, and I cannot separate those two from seismic alone" tells a drilling engineer exactly what the risk is.

The failure mode this module is built against is the confident single answer. A forward model that reproduces the observed amplitude feels like a result, and it is easy to stop there, because searching for rivals takes longer than finding the first fit and is much less satisfying. The search is the work. The first fit is where it starts.

There is a second, subtler problem, which is that the size of the solution set depends on the parameter ranges you allowed. Widen the shale range and more reservoirs become admissible; forbid low saturations and the fizz-gas ambiguity disappears by decree rather than by evidence. Those ranges encode real geological knowledge and they belong in the analysis — but they have to be stated, because a solution set is only as honest as the box it was searched in.

The counts on this page are a count of grid cells, not a probability. They say how many of the models this page can build are consistent with the measurement. Turning that into a probability would need a prior over the parameters that reflected real geology, which is a different and harder exercise, and the Method tab says more about why the distinction matters.

Exercises

  1. Find three matches that disagree about the fluid. With the noise on clean, press Show me another match repeatedly and record the porosity, fluid and saturation each time. Keep going until you have found a gas match, an oil match, and one with no hydrocarbon at all.
    HintAll three exist. With the noise clean this grid holds 6,795 matching models: 4,273 with gas, 2,456 with oil, and 66 with no hydrocarbon at all. The wet ones are high-porosity brine sands sitting under a fast shale, and the measurement cannot rule them out. Note what the counts do and do not say. They say a bright amplitude is much easier to produce with hydrocarbons than without, on this grid, which is why bright spots get drilled. They do not say the odds are 99 to 1, because the grid is not a prior and every cell in it was given equal weight by nobody in particular.
  2. Walk the porosity–saturation contour and note what does not change. In step 2, read the pairs off the contour: porosity 30% at full gas, then 31%, 32%, 34% at progressively lower saturation. Now read the Vp/Vs panel next to it.
    HintThe contour runs from 30% porosity at 100% gas saturation to 34% porosity at 10% saturation, and the amplitude is the same to within 0.002 the whole way. Vp/Vs across that span goes from 1.47 to 1.54 — a change of 0.07, against the 0.21 that separated the oil and brine sands in module 01. So the second measurement AVO provides would separate those two rocks, and would struggle with these. Knowing which ambiguities offset helps and which it does not is most of what AVO judgment consists of.
  3. Price the shale. In step 3, read the porosity range admitted when the shale velocity is known exactly, then the range when it is unknown across the full slider.
    HintOn this panel everything but the shale and the porosity is held at your settings. With the shale pinned at 2700 m/s the amplitude admits a single porosity, 30%. Let it range over ±400 m/s and the admissible band opens to 26–35%: nine porosity units of uncertainty, bought entirely by a rock nobody is trying to characterise. The two cards show the rocks at either end, and their traces are the same picture. Read this against exercise 4 of module 01, where the shale slider alone changed the reflection by a factor of 3.9: the same fact, seen from the inverse direction.
  4. Make the volumetrics wrong. In step 4, find the thinnest and thickest reservoirs that match, and work out the ratio of their pore volumes.
    HintThe thinnest that fits is 10 m at 28% porosity, tuned by a factor of about 1.25. The thickest is off the end of the slider, and that is the real answer: above the tuning thickness of 14.8 m the tuning factor is exactly one and stays there, so every greater thickness gives an identical amplitude. The measurement sets a lower bound on thickness and no upper bound at all. Across the range this page offers, pore volume — thickness times porosity — varies by at least 5.9 times, and in principle without limit. An amplitude map read as a thickness or quality map inherits that, and below tuning it inherits it backwards: the thinner reservoir is the brighter one.
  5. Find the unknown that will not go away. In step 5, switch on the shale, then the thickness, then the clay content, recording the survivor count and the saturation range at each step.
    HintThe count runs 6,795 with nothing known, 724 knowing the shale, 67 knowing the shale and the thickness, and 15 knowing all three — a factor of 453, which is a real return on outside information. Now read the saturation column: 0–100%, then 5–100%, then 5–100%, then 10–100%. It barely moves. Porosity narrows from 17–35% to 30–34% and Vp/Vs from 1.45–2.08 to 1.47–1.54, so the other unknowns really do collapse. The one question that decides whether the well pays is left almost exactly where it started. That is the honest summary of what a single amplitude carries, and it is why the next module goes looking for a second measurement.

Key points

  • The forward direction is exact and the inverse direction is not. A rock predicts an amplitude to as many decimals as you like. An amplitude admits a set of rocks, and the size of that set is the real result of the measurement.
  • Porosity and saturation trade off along a nearly flat contour. 30% porosity at full gas and 34% porosity at 10% gas produce the same amplitude. One is a discovery and the other is a dry hole, and no processing of that single amplitude separates them.
  • Half of the reflection belongs to the seal. Pinning the shale gives a single admissible porosity; letting it move ±400 m/s opens that to 26–35%. Uncertainty in a rock nobody is characterising converts into uncertainty in the one that matters, at a nearly linear exchange rate.
  • Tuning bounds thickness from below and not at all from above. A 10 m reservoir at 28% porosity matches the same amplitude as a 45 m one at 30%, because the thin one is tuned and the thick one is not. Above the tuning thickness every thickness gives the identical amplitude, so pore volume is unbounded — and below it, brighter means thinner.
  • Outside knowledge pays, and the payment is measurable. Knowing the shale removes nine tenths of the solutions; knowing the shale, thickness and clay together removes all but 15 of 6,795. That is a number to weigh against what the information costs.
  • Saturation is the unknown that survives everything. Through every stage of that collapse the admissible saturation stays essentially 0 to 100%. A single normal-incidence amplitude spends its information on impedance, and impedance stops responding to gas above a few percent.

MMethod, simplifications and references

What is computed, and where it departs from production software

The forward model is module 01's, unchanged: Voigt-Reuss-Hill mineral mixing, a Dvorkin-Nur soft-sand dry frame at 40% critical porosity and 23 MPa, Batzle-Wang fluids mixed by the Reuss average, Gassmann for the saturated modulus, and Castagna-Gardner for the bounding shale. Module 01's Method tab lists the assumptions in those steps and they all apply here. This module adds only the search.

The measurement. The observed amplitude is generated from a clean gas sand at 30% porosity, 45 m thick, under a 2700 m/s shale, with a 30 Hz Ricker. It is drawn noise-free and the noise setting is used only as the match tolerance, so that the counts quoted in the exercises are reproducible. On real data the noise also displaces the measured amplitude itself, which widens the solution set further than anything shown here.

The search grid. Porosity 5–35% in 1% steps, clay 0–30% in 10% steps, fluid brine, oil or gas, hydrocarbon saturation 5–100% in 5% steps, shale velocity 2300–3100 m/s in 100 m/s steps, thickness 10–55 m in 5 m steps. A hydrocarbon at zero saturation is the same rock as brine, so it is counted once rather than three times. That is 457,560 combinations, evaluated on every update.

Tuning is tabulated, not convolved. For a layer whose top and base reflections are equal and opposite, the peak amplitude is the reflection coefficient times a factor that depends only on the product of peak frequency and two-way time across the layer — not on the two separately. That factor is computed once into a table and interpolated, which is what makes a 383,904-point search fast enough to run while you drag a slider. The table is checked against directly convolved traces and against the closed form for its maximum, √6/2π ≈ 0.3898, where the layer thickness matches the wavelet's own peak-to-trough separation.

Counts are not probabilities. This is the most important caveat on the page. The survivor count is the number of cells in a hand-chosen grid, under an implicitly uniform weight, that reproduce the measurement. It is not the probability that the reservoir contains gas. Turning it into one would require a prior reflecting what is actually known about the basin, the depositional setting and the trap, and different priors give materially different answers from the same seismic. Read the counts as "this many distinct explanations exist", which is a statement about the measurement, and not as "this is how likely each is", which is a statement about geology that seismic alone cannot make.

Further departures from production practice.

  • The wavelet is known exactly, and fixed at 30 Hz. In practice the wavelet is estimated, and errors in its amplitude and phase map straight into amplitude errors that this page treats as zero.
  • One amplitude, one interface. Real interpretation uses the character of the whole event, its lateral behaviour, its conformance to structure, and the base reflection as well as the top. Each of those is additional information and each shrinks the solution set. The point here is what the single amplitude does and does not carry, not that interpreters have nothing else.
  • The two bounding shales are identical, so the base reflection is exactly the negative of the top and the tuning factor is the symmetric one. An asymmetric pair tunes differently and would need the full convolution rather than the table.
  • Normal incidence only. Every rock counted here is counted on one number. Module 03 adds the offset dimension, and the interesting question there is which of these ambiguities it removes — the porosity–saturation contour in step 2 suggests it will not remove that one.
  • No lateral information, no map. A single trace has no idea whether the anomaly conforms to a structural closure, which in practice is often worth more than the amplitude itself.
