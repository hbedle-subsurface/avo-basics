What all of this is for

This is the foundation module. It has no equations in it, and it assumes you have never met any of these words before. By the end you will know what a seismic trace is, what makes a reflection, what impedance means, what a gather is, and what AVO promises to do — which is what the other six modules take for granted from their first sentence.

1Seismic is an echo

Shout at the ground, listen for what comes back

A seismic survey is a very careful echo experiment. Someone makes a loud, sharp sound at the surface — an air gun at sea, a vibrating truck on land. The sound travels down through the rock. Wherever it crosses from one kind of rock into a different kind, a little of it bounces back up, and a microphone at the surface records what returns.

Two things get recorded for each echo. When it came back, which tells you how deep the boundary is. And how loud it was, which tells you something about the two rocks meeting at that boundary. This whole site is about that second number, and about how much it really tells you.

The picture on the left is the simplest possible version: one layer of rock buried in another. There are two boundaries — the top of the buried layer and its base — so the echo comes back twice. The recording of those echoes, drawn as a wiggly line down the page, is called a trace.

Notice that the trace is drawn with time running downward. That is a convention you will meet everywhere in seismic: deeper things arrive later, and later is further down the page. It takes a little getting used to and then becomes invisible.

2Hard rock, soft rock

What decides how loud the echo is

Why does any sound bounce back at all? Because the two rocks are different, and the sound notices the difference.

What it notices is a combination of two things: how fast sound travels in the rock and how heavy the rock is. Multiply those two together and you get a single number that geophysicists call the acoustic impedance, usually just "impedance". You can think of it as how stiff and heavy the rock feels to a passing sound wave. A dense, fast limestone has a high impedance. A light, slow, gas-filled sand has a low one.

Here is the rule, and it is the only rule in this module: the echo is loud when the two impedances are very different, and silent when they are the same. Not when the impedance is high. When it changes. Two identical rocks stacked on each other produce no echo at all, however remarkable each of them is.

There is one more thing the echo carries, which is its polarity — whether the wiggle kicks left or right. Going from a soft rock into a hard one gives one direction; hard into soft gives the other. Drag the sliders until the buried layer is softer than the rock above and watch the whole wiggle flip over.

That echo strength has a proper name, the reflection coefficient, and it is written as a fraction: 0.1 means a tenth of the sound came back. It runs from about −0.3 to +0.3 for ordinary rocks, and the minus sign just means the wiggle kicks the other way. Every other module on this site calls it R.

3Why it is a wiggle and not a spike

The sound you sent down has a shape

If the source made a perfect, instantaneous click, each echo would come back as a single sharp spike and seismic would be a much easier subject. Real sources cannot do that. What goes down is a short pulse with a shape to it — a little up-down-up wobble lasting a few hundredths of a second. That shape is called the wavelet.

Every echo comes back wearing that shape. So the trace is not a set of spikes, it is one copy of the wavelet per boundary, added together. That is all the word convolution means when you meet it later: put a copy of the wavelet at each echo and add them up.

Now the important consequence. The wavelet has a width. If two boundaries are closer together in time than that width, their two wavelets overlap and merge into one event, and you can no longer see them separately. Take the thickness slider down and watch it happen: two clean wiggles become one fat wiggle, and then the layer stops being resolvable at all.

The sharp pulse slider changes the wavelet's width. A sharper pulse — higher frequency — separates thinner layers. Notice what this means: change nothing about the rocks, change only the sound you sent down, and the picture changes. The trace is a picture of the earth taken with a particular instrument, not the earth itself.

4Not one microphone, but hundreds

Where the word "offset" comes from

So far there has been one source and one microphone in the same place, and the sound has gone straight down and straight back. A real survey does not work like that. There is one source and a long line of microphones stretching away from it, sometimes for several kilometres.

The distance from the source to a given microphone is called the offset. A microphone right next to the source is a near offset; one at the far end of the line is a far offset. Sound reaching a far microphone did not travel straight down — it went down at a slant, bounced off the boundary at an angle, and came back up at a slant.

Every microphone records its own trace, and if you gather together all the traces that bounced off the same spot on the boundary you get a gather: a set of traces of the same piece of geology, seen from increasingly slanted angles. That word — gather — is one of the most-used in the whole subject, and it just means that collection.

Usually all those traces get averaged together into one, which is called stacking, and the result is the seismic image everybody looks at. Stacking makes a much cleaner picture. It also throws away the thing this entire site is about, because the echo is not the same loudness on every trace in the gather, and that variation carries information.

5What AVO claims to do

The promise, stated the way it is usually taught

Now the whole idea can be stated in one paragraph. Amplitude versus offset — AVO — is the observation that the loudness of an echo changes as you move out across the gather, and the claim that how it changes tells you what is in the rock's pore space.

The reasoning goes like this. Gas is very light and very compressible. Put gas into a porous sand and the rock gets lighter and much easier to squeeze, so its impedance drops a long way and the echo gets loud — a bright spot. But gas does something else too: it changes how the rock responds to being twisted as well as squeezed, and a slanted bounce twists the rock where a straight-down bounce does not. So a gas-filled rock does not just give a loud echo, it gives an echo whose loudness changes with offset in a particular way.

That is the promise, and it is worth stating in its confident form because it is what you will hear: brine sands and gas sands behave differently across a gather, so AVO can tell you which one you are looking at. It is a real effect, it is used every day, and it has found a great deal of oil and gas.

The panel on the right shows it working. Same rock frame, same depth, same everything — one filled with brine, one filled with gas. The gathers do not look alike.

WWhy it matters

What this is used for, in one page

Seismic data is how anyone knows anything about rock that nobody has drilled. A well tells you everything about one spot a few inches across. Seismic tells you a little about everywhere, and the whole discipline is the business of turning that little into enough.

  • Finding things worth drilling. A well costs a great deal. Anything that improves the odds before spudding is worth a lot, and amplitude is one of the few clues about pore fluid that can be had without drilling.
  • Deciding where to put a well in a field already found. Amplitudes map out laterally, so they show where a reservoir thickens, thins or changes.
  • Monitoring a producing field. Shoot the same survey again years later. Where the fluid has moved, the amplitudes have changed, and you can see the effect of production.
  • Anything that needs the subsurface. Carbon storage, geothermal, groundwater and engineering all use the same physics. The word "reservoir" here is a habit of the oil industry, not a limit on the method.

There is one thing worth being clear about before you go further, because it shapes everything else. You are never measuring the rock. You are measuring an echo that a rock produced, through an instrument with its own character, with other rocks around it, and with noise on top. Several different rocks can produce the same echo, and no amount of care with the recording changes that.

That is not a flaw in seismic. It is what seismic is. The skill in interpretation lies in knowing which questions the echo can settle and which it cannot, and the six modules after this one are an attempt to make that concrete rather than a matter of experience.

If you take one thing from this module, take the rule from step 2: an echo tells you that two rocks are different, not what either of them is. Almost every mistake in amplitude interpretation is a version of forgetting that.

Exercises

  1. Make the echo disappear. Adjust the buried layer's speed and weight until the trace goes flat. How many different ways can you find to do it?
    HintThere are many, and that is the point. The echo vanishes whenever the two impedances match — whenever speed times weight is the same for both rocks. A fast light layer and a slow heavy one can both do it. So a flat trace does not mean there is nothing there; it means there is nothing different there. A reservoir with the same impedance as its cap rock is completely invisible to seismic, and they exist.
  2. Flip the polarity. Start with the buried layer harder than the rock above, then make it softer, and watch the wiggle.
    HintThe whole wiggle turns over. Going from soft into hard gives one direction, hard into soft gives the other, and the base of the layer always does the opposite of the top because you are crossing the same boundary the other way. This is why interpreters care so much about which way is "peak" and which is "trough" in a given dataset — get the convention backwards and every soft rock looks hard.
  3. Lose a layer by thinning it. Set the pulse to 30 Hz and take the thickness down from 80 m, watching the two echoes.
    HintSomewhere around 25 to 30 m the two echoes stop being separable and merge into one event. Below that you cannot measure the layer's thickness from the trace at all — and worse, the merged event is often louder than either echo alone, so a thin layer can look like a stronger reflector than a thick one. Now raise the pulse frequency and watch the two echoes separate again. Nothing about the rock changed.
  4. Watch the gather fan out. In step 4, set a large difference between the two rocks and look along the gather from left to right.
    HintThe echo is not the same loudness on every trace. That variation is the entire subject of AVO, and stacking — averaging the gather into a single trace — deliberately throws it away in exchange for a cleaner picture. Both things are true: the stack is the better image, and the gather has more information in it.
  5. See the claim work. In step 5, compare the brine and gas gathers on the near traces and then on the far traces.
    HintThey differ on the near traces because gas lowers the impedance, and they differ more on the far traces because gas also changes how the rock responds to a slanted bounce. That widening gap is the AVO signal. Everything after this module is about how big that gap really is, what else could produce it, and how confidently you could measure it on data that has noise in it.

Key points

  • Seismic is an echo experiment. A sharp sound goes down, a little bounces back from every boundary between different rocks, and a microphone records when it returned and how loud it was.
  • Impedance is speed times weight. It is how stiff and heavy a rock feels to a passing sound wave, and it is the only rock property a straight-down echo responds to.
  • An echo measures a difference, not a thing. Loud means the two rocks are very unlike each other. Two identical rocks give no echo, and two matching impedances make a real boundary invisible.
  • The trace is a wiggle because the pulse is. Every echo comes back wearing the shape of the sound you sent down, so two boundaries closer than that shape is wide merge into one event you cannot separate.
  • A gather is the same spot seen from many angles. Offset is the distance from the source to the microphone; far offsets bounce at a slant. Stacking averages them for a cleaner picture and throws the variation away.
  • AVO is the claim that the variation across the gather tells you about the pore fluid. It is a real effect and it works. How far it can be trusted is what the other six modules are for.

MMethod, simplifications and references

What is computed, and what has been left out on purpose

Everything on this page is computed, from the same code the other six modules use: exact reflection coefficients, a real Ricker wavelet, and traces built by convolution. Nothing is a drawing of what the answer ought to look like.

This module simplifies deliberately, and it is worth saying where. You set the buried layer's speed and weight directly here. In the real world you cannot: those come out of the rock's mineral grains, its pore space and its pore fluid, and working out how is a subject of its own. Module 01 does exactly that and hands you back the same two numbers you are setting by hand here.

The step 5 comparison is the one place that shortcut is dropped: those two gathers come from the full rock physics, with a real sand at 30% porosity filled first with brine and then with gas, because the whole point of the panel is that a fluid change produces both effects at once.

Other things left out on purpose:

  • No noise. Every trace here is perfectly clean, which no real trace is. Module 05 is entirely about what noise does.
  • Only two layers. Real sections have a boundary every few metres, and their echoes overlap with each other constantly.
  • The gather is already flattened, and the angles are computed with straight rays for simplicity. Module 03 shows that straight rays under-read the true angle by more than ten degrees at long offset, which matters a great deal later and not at all here.
  • No mode conversion, no multiples, no absorption, no anisotropy, and the echo strength is the plane-wave value rather than anything a real recording system would deliver.

If you want the terms in one place: impedance is speed times density; reflection coefficient is the fractional strength of an echo, written R; the wavelet is the shape of the pulse; convolution is putting a copy of the wavelet at every echo and adding them; offset is source-to-microphone distance; a gather is the traces from one spot at many offsets; stacking is averaging a gather into one trace; tuning is what happens when two echoes merge; and AVO is the study of how echo strength changes across a gather.
