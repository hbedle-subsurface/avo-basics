Module 01  ·  Foundations

A pulse, a boundary, an echo

This is the first module in the set. It assumes a first-year geology background and nothing else: no geophysics, no equations beyond dividing a distance by a speed. It covers what a seismic survey physically does, what a seismic trace is a record of, why a buried layer produces two echoes rather than one, how a thickness is obtained from a time difference, and how single traces line up into the sections that get interpreted.

1A seismic survey is an echo experiment

A pulse goes down, part of it comes back

A seismic survey begins with a short, sharp pulse of sound made at the surface. On land the source is usually a truck that vibrates the ground; at sea it is an air gun. The pulse travels downward through the rock.

Wherever the pulse crosses from one kind of rock into a different kind, part of it continues downward and part of it turns around and travels back up. The part that turns around is the reflection, or the echo. A receiver at the surface — a geophone on land, a hydrophone at sea — records the ground motion arriving back at the surface, over a period of a few seconds.

That recording is a seismic trace. It is a single record of ground motion against time, from one point at the surface. A trace is drawn as a wiggly line, and each place where the line departs from the center is an echo returning from a boundary somewhere below.

A boundary at 1200 m in rock with a velocity of 2400 m/s takes half a second to reach. The echo takes the same half second to come back, so it arrives at the surface one second after the pulse left. The pair of panels below traces that path and shows where the echo lands on the trace.

2The vertical axis is time

Two-way time, and why the display runs downward

A trace is recorded against time, not depth. The instrument has no way of knowing how far down a boundary is; it knows only when the echo came back. The quantity that a trace is plotted against is called two-way time — the time for the pulse to travel down and the echo to travel back — and it is almost always written in seconds or milliseconds.

Two-way time and depth are related through velocity. For a boundary at depth z beneath rock of velocity v, the two-way time is 2z/v. The factor of two is the round trip. Reading that relationship in the other direction, depth is v times two-way time divided by two, which requires a velocity that the trace itself does not supply.

The consequence is that a time section and a depth section are different pictures of the same ground. Two boundaries at the same depth arrive at different times if the rock above them has different velocities. The panels below hold the layer at a fixed depth and change only the velocity of the rock above it; the layer stays where it is and its echo moves.

Seismic displays are drawn with time increasing downward. Deeper boundaries return their echoes later, and later is further down the page, so the display has the same sense as a geological cross-section even though its vertical axis is not a depth.

Converting a time section to a depth section is a routine step in interpretation, and it is only as good as the velocity model it uses. Two interpreters with different velocity models produce different depths from identical seismic data.

3A layer has two boundaries

One echo from the top, one from the base

A single layer buried in another rock has two surfaces where the rock changes: the top and the base. The downgoing pulse crosses both, so two echoes return, separated in time by the round trip through the layer.

The two echoes have opposite signs. At the top the pulse passes from the slower rock into the faster layer; at the base it passes from the faster layer back into the slower rock. The same contrast is crossed in opposite senses, so the trace kicks one way at the top and the other way at the base. Which physical direction corresponds to which sign is a display convention, and it varies between datasets.

Counting boundaries and counting echoes is the most direct check available on an interpretation. A model with three layers has two internal boundaries and produces two echoes. An event on a trace that does not correspond to a boundary in the model has some other cause — noise, an echo that bounced more than once, or a boundary that was left out of the model.

4Thickness from a time difference

The measurement, and the number it depends on

The gap between the two echoes is a round trip through the layer, so it equals 2h/v, where h is the thickness and v is the velocity inside the layer. Rearranged, the thickness is v times the time gap divided by two.

The time gap is a measurement. The velocity is not: it comes from a well, from a velocity analysis of the seismic data, or from an assumption about what the layer is made of. A thickness read off a seismic section is therefore a measured time multiplied by an estimated velocity, and it inherits whatever error the velocity carries.

The right-hand panel shows thickness against time gap for three velocities. The lines diverge, and the divergence is proportional: a velocity that is ten percent too high gives a thickness that is ten percent too high, at every time gap. For a 40 ms gap the difference between a 2900 m/s layer and a 4500 m/s layer is about 32 m.

This is the point at which seismic measurement of thickness is at its most reliable, and it is worth noting the conditions. The layer here is thick enough that its two echoes are clearly separate. When a layer becomes thin, the two echoes overlap and the gap can no longer be measured at all. That limit is the subject of module 03.

5A section is a row of traces

Where the picture that gets interpreted comes from

Everything so far has concerned one trace, recorded at one point on the surface. A survey records at thousands of points. Setting those traces side by side, in the order they were recorded along the ground, and shading each one by its amplitude produces a seismic section: the image that appears in papers and on interpretation screens.

A section is therefore not a photograph of the subsurface and not a cross-section in the geological sense. It is a row of independent time recordings placed next to each other. The continuous horizons that appear across it emerge because adjacent traces are recorded over similar geology and their echoes arrive at similar times.

The model below replaces the flat layer with a lens: same top surface, but a base that rises toward each end until the layer pinches out. On the section the top of the lens is a flat event and the base is a curved one, and the two converge toward the edges.

Where the lens becomes thin, the two echoes stop appearing as separate events and merge into one. The location of that merge is a property of the recording, not of the geology: the lens continues to thin smoothly past the point where the section stops resolving it.

WWhy it matters

Where these five ideas are used

Mapping a horizon. The most common task performed on a seismic section is following one event across it and recording its arrival time at every trace. The result is a map in two-way time. Converting that map to depth requires a velocity model, and the depth map is a combined product of the seismic measurement and that model.

Estimating a reservoir volume. A volume is an area multiplied by a thickness. The area comes from a time map, the thickness from a time gap and a velocity. Both terms carry the velocity uncertainty, so a volume estimate is more sensitive to the velocity model than either measurement alone.

Tying seismic data to a well. A well reports depth; a seismic section reports time. Relating the two requires a velocity profile, usually measured in the well itself. Most disagreements between a well and a seismic interpretation are traced to that conversion rather than to the seismic data.

Recognizing what a section cannot show. A layer thinner than the resolution limit still exists and still holds fluid. The section does not distinguish it from a slightly thicker one, and it does not indicate that anything has been lost.

Exercises

Each answer is a number read off this page at the settings described.

  1. Move the layer without moving its echo. Set the depth to 1200 m and the velocity of the rock above to 2400 m/s, and note the two-way time. Then find a different depth and overburden velocity that put the echo at the same time.
    AnswerThe starting pair gives a two-way time of 1.000 s. Any pair with the same ratio does the same: 1000 m at 2000 m/s, or 1400 m at 2800 m/s. A single arrival time constrains the ratio of depth to velocity and does not constrain either one on its own. This is the reason a seismic time map is not a depth map and cannot be converted into one without independent velocity information.
  2. Separate the two effects on the trace. Change the velocity of the rock above and watch both echoes. Then change the velocity inside the layer and watch them again.
    AnswerThe overburden velocity shifts both echoes together and leaves the gap between them unchanged, because it affects only the travel time down to the layer. The layer velocity leaves the first echo where it is and moves the second one, because it affects only the round trip inside the layer. The arrival time of an event carries information about everything above it; the gap between two events carries information about what is between them.
  3. Convert a time gap to a thickness three times. Set the thickness to 60 m and the layer velocity to 3200 m/s. Record the time gap, then work out what thickness that same gap would imply at 2900 m/s and at 4500 m/s.
    AnswerThe gap is 37.5 ms. At 2900 m/s it implies 54 m, and at 4500 m/s it implies 84 m. The measurement did not change; the assumed velocity did. A thickness quoted from seismic data without a stated velocity is incomplete, and the range of plausible velocities for the layer sets the range of plausible thicknesses.
  4. Find where the lens stops being resolved. In step 5, set the thickness to 60 m and the layer velocity to 3200 m/s, then read the thinnest part still resolved and compare it with the thickest part of the lens.
    AnswerAround 21 m, against a maximum of 60 m, so ten of the sixty traces — five at each end — record a single merged event. The lens itself thins smoothly all the way to zero. Nothing on the section marks the position where the two events stopped being separate, so the boundary between the resolved and unresolved parts of the lens is invisible in the data that would be used to map it.
  5. Change the recording rather than the rock. Keep the lens fixed and raise the layer velocity from 2900 to 4500 m/s, watching the merge point on the section.
    AnswerThe merged zone widens as the velocity rises, because a faster layer takes less time to cross and a given thickness therefore produces a smaller time gap. The geological lens is identical in every case. Two sections of the same body can disagree about where it pinches out, and neither is wrong about the data it recorded.

Key points

  • A seismic survey is an echo experiment. A short pulse travels down, part of it reflects at every boundary between different rocks, and a receiver at the surface records what returns.
  • A trace is one record of ground motion against time from one surface position. Each departure from the center line is an echo from a boundary below.
  • The vertical axis is two-way time, not depth. Two-way time is 2z/v, and converting it to depth requires a velocity that the seismic recording does not itself supply.
  • Each boundary produces one echo. A layer has a top and a base, so it produces two, and they have opposite signs because the same contrast is crossed in opposite senses.
  • Thickness is a measured time multiplied by an assumed velocity. The time gap is 2h/v; the velocity comes from a well, a velocity analysis or an assumption, and its error passes directly into the thickness.
  • A section is a row of independent traces placed side by side. Continuous horizons appear because adjacent traces record similar geology, not because the section is a picture of the ground.
  • Below a certain thickness the two echoes merge and the layer stops being separable. The layer is still there, and the section gives no indication that anything was lost.

MMethod, simplifications and references

What is computed, and what is left out on purpose

Everything on this page is computed from the four controls. Reflection coefficients come from the acoustic impedance contrast, traces are built by placing a Ricker wavelet at each arrival time and adding, and the section in step 5 is sixty such traces computed independently. No panel is a drawing of what the answer ought to look like.

Simplifications made deliberately in this module:

  • Density is held at 2.3 g/cm³ in all three layers, so the reflection strength here follows the velocity contrast alone. Real rocks vary in density as well, and module 02 puts both properties back.
  • The buried layer is always faster than the rock enclosing it. The velocity sliders do not overlap. This fixes the sign of the two echoes and keeps the module on timing. Module 02 removes the restriction, at which point either sign becomes possible.
  • The source and receiver are at the same place and the sound travels straight down and straight back. Real surveys record at many source-receiver separations, which is where modules 07 and 08 begin.
  • The wavelet is a 30 Hz Ricker, fixed. It is not adjustable here because its width is the subject of module 03. Its width is what sets the merge point in step 5.
  • No noise, and no multiples, mode conversions, absorption or anisotropy. Every trace is a clean sum of two arrivals.
  • The velocity above the layer is constant. Velocity generally increases with depth in a sedimentary section, which bends ray paths; that becomes relevant in module 07 and is irrelevant for the vertical paths used here.

The resolution limit quoted in step 5 is the peak-to-trough separation of the Ricker wavelet, √6 / (2πf), which is 13.0 ms at 30 Hz. A time gap smaller than that produces a single merged event. This is one of several defensible definitions of seismic resolution, and module 03 compares them; they differ from each other by more than the precision to which any of them can be applied.

Terms introduced here: a reflection is the part of a pulse that turns around at a boundary; a trace is one record of ground motion against time; two-way time is the down-and-back travel time; a section is a row of traces displayed side by side; and a layer is resolved when its top and base appear as separate events.
