What survives

Five modules of losses, ambiguities and error bars. This one adds them up. It counts how many rocks are consistent with one measurement, how many the second measurement removes, which question neither of them can answer, and it ends with a form of words you could actually defend in a meeting.

1Where the information went

Every step, and what each one cost

A rock has many independent properties. A seismic amplitude has one number, or two if you have offset. The difference has to go somewhere, and the earlier modules found out where. The card on the left collects it, with the size of each loss measured rather than described.

Read down it and notice that the losses are of different kinds. Some are collapses: several numbers becoming one, as when velocity and density become an impedance. Some are saturations: a property that stops responding once it has changed a little, as gas saturation does. Some are blurs: the wavelet in time, the noise in amplitude. And one is a confusion: half the reflection belongs to a rock you were not asking about.

Only the blurs get better with better data. The collapses and the saturation are properties of the physics and no amount of acquisition or processing touches them.

2What one number leaves you

Module 02's count, now with a real error bar on it

Module 02 counted the rocks consistent with a measured amplitude, using a tolerance picked to represent the noise. Now that tolerance can be computed properly: module 05 gives the uncertainty in a fitted intercept from the angle range, the fold and the noise, and the set of admissible rocks is everything within two of those standard deviations.

The panel shows the survivors as a population — what porosities, what fluids, what saturations. The count is large, and the important thing about it is not the number itself but which of its dimensions are wide and which are narrow.

Change the noise and the whole set breathes. Change the mute and it breathes again. Neither of them changes the geology by so much as a percent of porosity.

3What the second number buys

The whole case for AVO, as a number

Now require the gradient to match as well, within its own two standard deviations. The surviving set shrinks. How much it shrinks is the entire practical case for doing AVO, and it is worth having as a figure rather than a conviction.

At moderate noise the gradient removes 55% of what the intercept left. That is a real and useful return. At high noise it removes 25%, because the gradient's error bar has grown so wide that it barely constrains anything — which is module 05's result arriving in a form you can put in a budget.

Then look at which dimensions shrank. Vp/Vs narrows sharply, which is exactly what module 03 predicted: offset measures shear. Porosity narrows a little. And saturation does not move at all. The second measurement buys you lithology and fluid type; it does not buy you how much.

5What you can honestly say

Generated from the surviving set, not from a template

The statement below is assembled from the analysis you have just run. Each clause is backed by a count or a range from the surviving models: nothing in it is asserted that the numbers do not support, and nothing that they do support is left out.

Change the noise, the mute or the fold and watch the wording change with them. That responsiveness is the point. What you can defend depends on how well you measured, and it should be re-derived rather than remembered.

Notice the shape of it. There is usually something firm to say about impedance and about the presence of a hydrocarbon. There is usually nothing to say about saturation. And there is almost always a caveat about the seal, because half of every amplitude belongs to a rock nobody characterised.

WWhy it matters

Using a method whose limits you know

The point of all six modules is not that AVO is unreliable. It is that AVO answers some questions well, some badly and some not at all, and that which is which can be worked out in advance rather than discovered after a well.

  • Ask the question the data can answer. "Is there a hydrocarbon here" is often answerable. "How much" usually is not. Framing a prospect around the second one guarantees a disappointing result no matter how good the processing is.
  • Compute the value of the gradient before paying for it. Step 3 puts a number on what the second measurement removes. At high noise it removes a fifth of the solutions, which may not justify the work.
  • Spend on what narrows the set. Step 5 ranks the candidates. Very often the cheapest useful measurement is a constraint on the seal rather than anything about the reservoir.
  • Report a set, not a value. Every number in a quantitative interpretation is a representative of a set of possibilities. Quoting the representative without the set is the single most common way these methods mislead.

The habit that survives all of this is simple and slightly uncomfortable: having found a model that explains your data, go and look for the others. The first fit feels like an answer, and it is only a member of a set whose size you have not yet checked. Everything in these six modules is an elaboration of that.

It is worth saying plainly that this site is a model, and inherits the weakness it keeps pointing at. The rock physics is one choice among several, the noise is simpler than real noise, nothing here is spatial, and a real interpreter has structural conformance, well control and a hundred other things this page does not. The numbers are honest about the parameter space they searched. That space is not the earth.

If one sentence is worth carrying away from the whole set: an amplitude constrains a contrast, not a reservoir, and the size of the set it leaves you is computable before you drill.

Exercises

  1. Price the gradient. In step 3, read how many models survive the intercept alone and how many survive both numbers, at moderate noise and then at noisy.
    HintAt moderate noise 441 models match the intercept and 198 match both, so the gradient removes 55% of them. At high noise 1,156 match the intercept and 870 match both — the gradient removes only 25%. Same rocks, same acquisition geometry, same physics. What changed is how well the gradient could be measured, and module 05 explains precisely why. If a project cannot afford the processing to get the noise down, the AVO may be worth much less than the proposal assumed.
  2. Find the dimension that never narrows. Still in step 3, compare the Vp/Vs range and the saturation range before and after the gradient is applied.
    HintAt moderate noise Vp/Vs goes from 1.46–2.08 to 1.46–1.60, a real narrowing, and it is why the hydrocarbon-bearing fraction goes from 98% to 100%: offset measures shear, and the wet rocks are the ones with high Vp/Vs. Saturation goes from 0–100% to 10–100%, and the only thing that changed is that the wet rocks were removed. Among the models that do contain a hydrocarbon, every saturation from 10% to 100% still fits exactly as well. That is the fizz problem from modules 02 and 03 arriving at its final form: no measurement on this page, at any noise level, can tell a commercial column from a residual one.
  3. Watch the mute change what you can conclude. In step 5, set the widest angle to 15°, read the statement, then take it to 45° and read it again.
    HintAt a 15° mute the gradient removes 4% of the models the intercept left — its error bar is so wide that it constrains almost nothing, and the statement drops back to what an intercept alone can support. At 30° it removes 55%. At 45° it removes 75%. Nothing about the earth changed between the two readings. This is the most direct demonstration in the whole site that a processing parameter decides what you are allowed to conclude.
  4. Take each trap in turn. In step 4, work through all four and note which module first established each one.
    HintBright means gas fails because a gas sand at 20% porosity gives an intercept of −0.004 and is invisible — module 01, exercise 2. Dim means wet fails for the same reason from the other side. A lot of gas fails because 10% saturation produces two thirds of the full amplitude effect — module 01, exercise 3, and module 02's contour. Class III means gas fails because the same gas sand is Class I, II or III depending only on the shale above it — module 04, exercise 5. Four rules, four counterexamples, all built from the same physics rather than from anecdotes.
  5. Write the statement yourself, then compare. Set the noise to moderate and the mute to 30°, decide what you would tell a colleague, then open step 5.
    HintThe defensible version is close to: the impedance is constrained to a band; the response is very hard to produce without a hydrocarbon on this parameter grid; the fluid is more likely gas than oil on the gradient; the saturation is entirely unconstrained between about 5% and 100%; and the porosity depends on an assumption about the shale that has not been tested. Most people's first draft is firmer than that on saturation and vaguer than that on the shale, which is exactly the pair of errors this site was built to correct.

Key points

  • The losses are of different kinds and only some are fixable. Collapses like velocity and density into impedance, and the saturation of the gas effect, are physics. The blurs — the wavelet, the noise — are the only ones better data improves.
  • One number leaves a large set. With a properly computed tolerance, 441 rocks out of the 13,020 searched match the measured intercept at moderate noise, spanning porosities from 19% to 35% and every saturation from zero to full.
  • The second number removes about half of them, and only when you can measure it. The gradient cuts 441 to 198 at moderate noise and 1,156 to 870 when noisy — 55% against 25%. Widen the mute instead and it cuts 75%; narrow it to 15° and it cuts 4%. The value of AVO is proportional to how well the gradient is determined.
  • What offset buys is Vp/Vs, and therefore fluid type. The admitted Vp/Vs range narrows from 1.46–2.08 to 1.46–1.60, and the hydrocarbon-bearing fraction goes from 98% to 100%.
  • Saturation is untouched by all of it. Among the models that contain a hydrocarbon, before and after the gradient and at every noise level, the admitted saturation runs from 10% to 100%. Nothing in normal-incidence or offset-dependent amplitude separates a commercial column from a residual one.
  • Every rule of thumb has a computable counterexample. Bright is not gas, dim is not wet, a strong anomaly is not a lot of gas, and Class III is not a rock type — the same sand moves through three classes on the shale slider alone.

MMethod, simplifications and references

What is computed, and where it departs from production software

Everything here is built from the earlier modules. The rock physics is module 01's, the reflection coefficients are exact Zoeppritz as in module 03, the intercept and gradient are fitted as in module 04, and their uncertainties come from module 05's closed form. Each Method tab lists its own assumptions and all of them apply here.

The tolerances are computed, not chosen. A model is kept if its intercept is within two standard deviations of the measured one, and — in step 3 — its gradient likewise. Those standard deviations follow from the angle range, the number of traces and the noise level on the sliders, so moving any of those genuinely changes what is admissible rather than just relabelling it.

The search grid is porosity 5–35% in 1% steps, clay 0–30% in 10% steps, fluid brine, oil or gas, hydrocarbon saturation 10–100% in 10% steps, and shale velocity 2300–3100 m/s in 200 m/s steps, with a hydrocarbon at zero saturation counted once as brine rather than three times. The fit for each model uses eight angles rather than sixteen, which changes the recovered gradient by about 0.001 and makes the sweep fast enough to run while a slider moves.

Counts are not probabilities. This is the same caution as module 02's and it matters more here, because the closing statement is generated from the counts. The survivor count is the number of cells in a hand-chosen grid, weighted equally, that reproduce the measurement. Turning it into a probability needs a prior that reflects the basin, the depositional setting and the trap, and different priors give materially different answers from identical seismic. Read every count as "this many distinct explanations exist", which is a statement about the measurement, and never as "this is how likely each is", which is a statement about geology that seismic alone cannot make.

Where this departs from production practice.

  • Nothing is spatial. This is the largest omission on the page. Real interpretation gains enormously from an anomaly conforming to structure and repeating over an area, and none of that is available to a single-gather analysis.
  • No well control, no seismic inversion, no other attributes, all of which narrow the set further and are the reason quantitative interpretation is worth doing at all.
  • The truth is known to the page and hidden from the analysis. That is a luxury: on real data nobody can check the answer, which makes the discipline of counting alternatives more important rather than less.
  • Thickness is fixed at 45 m and tuning is therefore not part of the search here, though module 02 showed it widening the admissible set considerably.
  • The generated statement is a teaching device. It is assembled from the counts by a fixed set of rules. It is not a report, and a real one would carry the geological reasoning that this page has no access to.
